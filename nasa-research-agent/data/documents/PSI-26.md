# Burning and Suppression of Solids (BASS)

Fire behaves differently when gravity is taken away. On Earth, the hot gases rising from a flame drive the buoyant flows that continuously feed fresh oxygen to the combustion zone and carry away combustion products — a process so fundamental to terrestrial fire that most fire safety standards are built around it without question. The BASS investigation challenged that assumption directly, by burning real spacecraft materials aboard the ISS for durations long enough to reveal how solid-fuel flames genuinely behave in the absence of buoyancy. Rather than studying an idealized thin-film sample in a brief drop-tower experiment, BASS worked with practical material thicknesses and geometries, and then tested whether a nitrogen-based suppressant could extinguish those flames — giving NASA one of its first systematic looks at solid-material fire suppression under true microgravity conditions.

## 2. Research Objectives

Two interconnected problems motivated the BASS investigation.

The first concerned **material flammability standards**. NASA's current process for selecting spacecraft materials uses Test 1 of NASA-STD-6001 — a standard 1-g upward flame spread test — as the primary flammability screen. This test is straightforward to run, but it is conducted in normal gravity, and its results do not necessarily predict how the same material will behave when buoyancy is absent. Ground-based microgravity analogs (drop towers, parabolic aircraft) exist, but they provide only a few seconds of reduced gravity — far too short to observe extended flame spread over thicker, geometrically realistic samples. BASS aimed to begin bridging these three levels of testing by observing flame behavior over longer timescales, on more practical materials, in the actual microgravity environment for which spacecraft materials are ultimately being selected. The investigation also examined **wake flames** — flames burning in the aerodynamic shadow of an upstream object — which are relevant to real fire scenarios in tight, obstructed spacecraft compartments.

The second objective focused on **fire suppression**. The investigation tested whether an inert gaseous extinguishing agent, chemically similar to the nitrogen-based suppression system actually installed on the ISS, could reliably extinguish flames burning over different materials, in different geometries, with varying levels of fuel pre-heating and at different airflow conditions. This provided a direct, operationally relevant test of suppression effectiveness in the target environment.

## 3. Experimental Approach

### Facility and Containment

All flight experiments were performed inside the **Microgravity Science Glovebox** aboard the ISS — a contained, sealed working volume that allows crewmembers to handle experimental hardware while preventing any released material from escaping into the station atmosphere. The MSG provided structural containment and power, while BASS-specific hardware managed airflow, ignition, and data collection.

### Fuel Materials and Sample Geometries

The investigation used several distinct fuel materials:

- **75% cotton / 25% fiberglass fabric** (by mass) — a composite blend that burns but does not sustain full flame-through, allowing controlled combustion tests.
- **Nomex fabric** — a flame-resistant aramid fiber used widely in protective clothing and spacecraft soft goods.
- **Ultem** — a high-performance polyetherimide thermoplastic used in aerospace components.
- **PMMA (polymethyl methacrylate) spheres** — in 1 cm and 2 cm diameters, providing a geometrically simple, well-characterized fuel form.
- **SIBAL** — cylindrical and spherical configurations also appeared in the experimental table.

Flat fabric samples were placed inside a small flow duct with a **76 mm × 76 mm cross-section** that directed controlled airflow across the fuel surface. Additional sample shapes including spheres and cylinders were tested to explore geometry effects on flame behavior.

Ignition was initiated at one end of each sample using a **Kanthal wire (29-gauge) resistance igniter**.

### Airflow Configurations

Airflow was supplied by a variable-speed fan capable of velocities up to **40 cm/s**, directed across the fuel surface in one of two configurations:

- **Concurrent flow** — airflow traveling in the same direction as flame spread, which tends to extend the flame and promote faster spread by pre-heating unburned fuel ahead of the front.
- **Opposed flow** — airflow traveling toward the advancing flame front, which limits the heat reaching unburned fuel and can eventually lead to blow-off extinction at sufficient velocities.

This distinction is fundamental to microgravity fire research: on Earth, buoyancy produces an effective upward flow that always creates a concurrent-flow condition for upward-spreading flames. In microgravity, the absence of this natural flow means that the direction and magnitude of ventilation-driven flow can be precisely controlled, allowing the two regimes to be studied independently.

### Measurements and Diagnostics

Flames were observed from two angles — **top profile** and **edge profile** — to characterize three-dimensional flame structure. Recorded quantities included:

- Flame size and shape
- Flame color (indicative of temperature and soot loading)
- Flame spread rate along the fuel surface
- Occurrence and conditions of flame quenching
- Response to suppressant injection

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The driving hypothesis behind BASS was that **solid materials in microgravity, given adequate ventilation, burn at least as well as — and potentially more vigorously than — the same materials under identical conditions in normal gravity** (same pressure, oxygen concentration, and temperature). This runs counter to an intuitive assumption that reduced gravity must somehow weaken fire; in fact, on thin materials the absence of buoyancy can produce slower, cooler flames. For thicker materials, however, the longer residence time of hot gases near the fuel surface may sustain or intensify combustion.

The critical practical constraint the hypothesis exposed was one of timescale: testing anything beyond the thinnest fuel samples requires extended microgravity duration. Drop towers deliver only a few seconds; parabolic aircraft provide roughly twenty seconds of reduced gravity per maneuver. Neither is sufficient to allow a flame burning over a thick solid fuel to reach a quasi-steady state. BASS used the ISS to access minutes rather than seconds, making it possible to test the hypothesis in a regime that ground-based analogs simply cannot reach.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

> **Note:** The PSI record provided for this analysis does not include a compiled results summary or quantitative output table. The experimental table shows 5 of 123 test entries, and detailed results are distributed across the associated publications. The contributions described here are drawn from the objectives, approach, and listed publications within the PSI record. No external results have been introduced.

### Bridging the Normal-Gravity and Microgravity Flammability Gap

BASS generated flame spread data over practical fuel thicknesses and geometries for durations unavailable to any ground-based microgravity platform. This data directly addresses the gap between NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 performance and actual microgravity behavior, providing an empirical reference point that can either validate or challenge the current material-selection screening process.

### Opposed-Flow Flame Spread Characterization

Associated publications document systematic investigation of opposed-flow flame spread in both microgravity and normal gravity, including the role of boundary layers, blow-off extinction thresholds, and the thermal regime governing flame spread rate. The microgravity environment allowed the aerodynamic boundary layer contribution to be isolated from buoyancy effects — a separation that is not possible in ground experiments — leading to a clearer physical interpretation of the spread mechanism.

### Radiation as a First-Order Factor

Several publications arising from BASS focused on **radiative heat emission from the flame** as a critical variable. In microgravity, without buoyant convection to dominate energy transport, radiation becomes comparatively more important in governing both flame structure and extinction. BASS data were used to characterize this radiation signature and to build first-order models of heat release in spacecraft fires, with direct application to extending NASA-STD-6001 Test 2 (cone calorimeter) data toward performance-based material selection.

### Nitrogen Suppression Effectiveness

Testing nitrogen as a flame suppressant under these conditions provided a direct experimental link to ISS operational suppression capabilities. Results from these tests contribute to understanding the suppressant concentrations and delivery conditions required to extinguish solid-material fires in microgravity — information that cannot be reliably inferred from ground-based suppression data alone.

### Combustion Model Validation

The simpler, well-controlled flow environment inside the MSG — particularly in the opposed-flow configuration — made BASS data well-suited for validating detailed computational combustion models. Validated models can then be extended to the more complex flow environments characteristic of normal-gravity fires, increasing their practical utility for both spacecraft fire safety analysis and terrestrial combustion research.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

**Category: Suppression / Fundamental**

BASS addresses fire safety at multiple levels simultaneously, covering both the fundamental characterisation of how solid materials burn and the applied question of whether a specific suppression agent can extinguish those fires.

- **Material flammability standards:** The investigation directly targets a known weakness in current spacecraft material selection — that NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 is a 1-g test applied to a low-gravity problem. By generating extended-duration microgravity burning data on practical materials, BASS provides the evidence needed to evaluate whether current standards are conservative, adequate, or potentially non-conservative in specific scenarios.
- **Wake flame behavior:** Fires burning in the aerodynamic wake of another object — behind equipment racks, inside cable bundles, or in partially accessible compartments — are among the most difficult to detect and suppress. BASS examined this geometry explicitly, filling a gap in the experimental record.
- **Suppression agent performance:** The direct testing of nitrogen-based suppression against burning solid materials in controlled microgravity conditions gives fire safety engineers quantitative information about suppression efficacy that is operationally relevant to both current ISS operations and future crewed spacecraft.
- **Radiation-based fire detection:** By characterising the radiative heat output from flames in microgravity, BASS contributes to the physical basis for radiation-sensitive fire detectors appropriate for spacecraft environments, where convective smoke transport may differ significantly from normal-gravity patterns.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

### Microgravity Flammability Is Not Conservative by Default
The BASS hypothesis — that materials burn at least as well in microgravity as in normal gravity — challenges the assumption that Earth-based flammability tests provide a conservative upper bound for spacecraft fire risk. For materials beyond the thinnest samples, this assumption may not hold, and BASS provides the experimental grounding to evaluate it properly.

### Extended Burn Time Changes the Physics
The difference between a few seconds of drop-tower microgravity and several minutes aboard the ISS is not merely quantitative. Flames over thicker fuels need time to establish quasi-steady spread rates, develop soot structures, and reach thermal equilibrium with the surrounding atmosphere. Results obtained only in the extended-duration ISS environment reflect a qualitatively different combustion regime from what brief ground-based analogs can capture.

### Boundary Layer Effects Are Separable from Buoyancy
Opposed-flow flame spread experiments in microgravity allow the aerodynamic boundary layer effect on flame heat transfer to be isolated cleanly, because buoyant flow — which is always present in normal gravity and confounds boundary layer analysis — is absent. This separation produced more physically interpretable results and cleaner model validation than is achievable on the ground.

### Radiation Becomes a First-Order Combustion Variable
In the absence of dominant convective heat transfer, radiant emission from the flame controls a larger share of the energy balance. BASS flame radiation measurements provide quantitative inputs for spacecraft fire models that cannot be derived from normal-gravity experiments, where convection masks the radiation contribution.

### Operational Suppression Requires Microgravity-Specific Testing
Nitrogen suppression of solid-material fires in microgravity cannot be reliably characterized using ground-based suppression tests alone, because buoyancy affects both the flame structure being suppressed and the transport of suppressant gas through and around the flame. BASS established a direct experimental connection between operational ISS suppression capability and actual microgravity flame extinction behavior.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

### Material Certification for Future Spacecraft
BASS results directly inform the conversation about whether NASA-STD-6001 Test 1, in its current 1-g form, is an appropriate gateway for spacecraft material approval. If the investigation demonstrates that certain materials certified under the current standard burn more vigorously in microgravity than the test suggests, the certification process may need revision before those materials are used in future crewed vehicles — including the Lunar Gateway, Artemis surface habitats, and Mars transit spacecraft.

### ISS Fire Suppression Confidence
Because the nitrogen suppressant tested in BASS closely mirrors the agent available on the actual ISS, the results provide real operational value for understanding the limits and conditions under which the station's suppression system can be expected to function effectively against solid-material fires.

### Wake Flame Scenarios in Tight Compartments
Spacecraft interiors are dense with equipment, cabling, and structural elements that create aerodynamic wakes. A fire starting in an inaccessible location — behind a rack, inside a conduit chase, or within a bundle of cables — may burn primarily in a wake configuration. BASS is one of the few experimental programs to have directly studied this geometry in microgravity, providing data relevant to fire scenario planning for both current and future crewed vehicles.

### Performance-Based Material Selection
The radiation and heat-release data from BASS support a shift from pass/fail material certification toward **performance-based selection**, in which the actual heat release rate of a material in a realistic fire scenario is quantified and used to predict fire growth, detector response times, and suppression agent requirements. This approach is more flexible and potentially more accurate than a single binary test outcome.

## 9. Earth Benefits

### Validation of Combustion Models for Terrestrial Use
The well-controlled, buoyancy-free flow environment of the MSG allowed combustion models to be validated against experimental data with a clarity not achievable in normal-gravity tests. Once validated against the simpler microgravity case, these models can be extended to more complex flow environments relevant to Earth-based combustion in industrial burners, building fires, and confined-space fire scenarios.

### Improving Standards for Confined-Space Fires
The BASS findings on suppression effectiveness and flame spread in controlled, low-ventilation conditions are relevant to fire safety in confined terrestrial environments — submarine compartments, aircraft cargo bays, data center aisles, and enclosed industrial facilities — where air supply is limited and suppression agent concentration management is critical.

### Radiative Heat Transfer in Building Fire Models
The characterisation of radiative heat emission from solid-material flames without the confounding influence of buoyant convection contributes to the physical understanding of radiative heat transfer in fire dynamics. This has applicability to building fire modeling, where radiation governs ignition of objects at a distance from the primary flame and drives fire spread in ways that purely convection-focused models underestimate.

### Advanced Textile and Material Flammability Assessment
The testing of cotton-fiberglass blends, Nomex, and Ultem in controlled microgravity conditions contributes reference data for material scientists developing flame-resistant textiles and structural components. Understanding how these materials behave across gravity levels provides a more complete picture of their combustion characteristics.

## 10. Limitations and Open Questions

- **No compiled results summary is included in the PSI record.** The experimental table shows 5 of 123 tests. Detailed quantitative results — spread rates, extinction conditions, suppression concentrations — are contained in external publications rather than consolidated within the PSI entry.
- **No modeling table is associated with this investigation**, and no Read Me file is available within the PSI record, limiting the contextual information accessible from the PSI entry alone.
- **Wake flame geometry:** While wake flames are listed as an objective, the extent to which the experimental table captures dedicated wake flame tests versus standard surface spread tests is not visible from the partial dataset provided.
- **Fuel preheating effects:** Preheating is mentioned as a variable in the suppression objective, but the experimental conditions for preheated tests and their influence on suppression effectiveness are not quantified in the supplied material.
- **Generalizability to thicker and more complex geometries:** Even with ISS access, the MSG volume constrains sample sizes. How the findings scale to larger fuel masses or more complex three-dimensional geometries representative of real spacecraft fires remains a question beyond the experimental scope.
- **Transition between opposed and concurrent flow regimes:** The practical threshold at which one regime gives way to the other — particularly near blow-off extinction — depends on material properties and flow history in ways that may require additional test coverage to fully characterise.
- **Long-term implications for NASA-STD-6001 revision:** Whether BASS data is sufficient to drive a formal revision of the current material standard, or whether additional ISS investigations (such as PSI-107 and PSI-25) are needed before a standards update can be justified, remains an open procedural and scientific question.
