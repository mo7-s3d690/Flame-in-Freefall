# Utilization of the Smoke Aerosol Measurement Experiment Data for Advanced Modeling and Simulation of Smoke Generation in Micro-Gravity

Flight experiments can tell you what smoke looks like in orbit, but they cannot easily tell you why. This investigation took the opposite route. Instead of flying hardware, it built a numerical model of smoke forming above a heated sample, then ran the same scenario with and without gravity to see which fluid dynamic effects change the resulting particles. It is a computational companion to the Smoke Aerosol Measurement Experiment (PSI-102).

## 2. Research Objectives

The aim was to use numerical simulation to study, from a fundamental standpoint, how gravity affects the growth of smoke produced by pyrolysis. Two things were sought: a quantitative picture of how microgravity smoke differs from smoke formed under Earth gravity, and an identification of the fluid dynamic mechanisms responsible for those differences.

The need comes from the fact that experiments in orbit are limited in number and cannot probe every condition. A validated model could fill in what hardware cannot.

## 3. Experimental Approach

No physical experiment was performed. The work is a simulation study.

The team simulated the formation and growth of dibutyl-phthalate (DBP) particles in both Earth gravity and microgravity, using direct numerical simulation with a Navier-Stokes nodal approach. The smoke source was pyrolysis vapor expelled from a heated sample, in a geometry similar to the one used in the SAME flight experiments.

Two cross-flow velocities were considered, both low enough that buoyancy cannot be ignored when gravity is present. The simulations were used to assess how particle size distributions depend on thermophoresis, particle settling, and flow geometry. The final report is described as presenting the simulation and modeling methodology, then the flow geometry and parameter space, then a comparison of wake geometry at the lower of the two flow speeds. Instantaneous scalar fields were used to show how particles accumulate and grow in the buoyant and non-buoyant wakes downstream of the hot vapor source.

The supplied material does not give the numerical values of the two flow speeds, the grid, the solver details, or the particle model parameters. There are no sensors or instruments, since nothing was measured physically.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The stated expectation is that removing buoyancy reduces mixing and heat transfer. That would significantly change the time-temperature history of the combustion vapors, which act as particle precursors, and alter particle trajectories, and so change the make-up of the smoke. The source adds that the chemical composition of the burning material also influences the smoke produced.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

**Experimentally observed findings.** None. This was not an experimental study.

**Computational findings.** The supplied material describes what was simulated and examined but reports no numerical outcomes. It does not state, for example, whether particles were larger or smaller in microgravity or by how much. The final report is mentioned, but its conclusions are not included.

**Scientific interpretation.** The description notes that wake geometry differed because of buoyancy at the lower flow speed. It does not explain the consequence for particle growth.

**Potential applications.** The source states that models of this kind can be used to determine how temperature, gravity, and material properties affect smoke, which it expects to lead to better smoke detection devices. This is a stated expectation.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

The relevance is to smoke detection. Detectors respond to particle properties, and a model that predicts those properties under different gravity levels, temperatures, and materials could inform detector design and evaluation. The source also links this to preventing fires that cause loss of materiel and life.

Material flammability is touched on only through the statement that burning material composition affects smoke characteristics. Ignition, extinction, oxygen effects, and suppression are not addressed in the supplied text.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

**Buoyancy shapes the smoke's thermal history.** The central idea is that gravity-driven flow alters how fast vapor cools and mixes, which sets the conditions for particle formation. This is the hypothesis, and the supplied text gives no confirmation.

**Wake structure is a candidate mechanism.** Comparing buoyant and non-buoyant wakes downstream of the source is the route chosen to identify what causes the differences.

**Several transport effects compete.** Thermophoresis, particle settling, and flow geometry were each assessed for their influence on size distributions, which implies that no single effect was assumed to dominate.

**Material matters.** Smoke character depends on what is burning, so conclusions drawn from one material may not carry over to another.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration
