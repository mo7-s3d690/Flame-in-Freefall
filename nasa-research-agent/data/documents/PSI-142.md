# Quantitative Studies of Cool Flame Transitions at Radiation / Stretch Extinction Using Counterflow Flames

A flame can die in two very different ways: it can be blown out by too much flow, or it can lose so much heat to radiation that it can no longer sustain itself. Add low-temperature chemistry to the picture and a third possibility appears, where a hot flame gives way to a faint cool flame instead of going out completely. This project mapped out where those transitions happen, using simulations and ground-based counterflow flames, and built directly on extinction data from the Flame Extinguishment (FLEX) experiments on the ISS.

## 2. Research Objectives

The grant used FLEX data from the PSI system as a starting point and set out to run new simulations and ground-based experiments with ozone-sensitized counterflow flames. Five goals are listed. The first was a new diagram of the flammability limits of diffusion cool flames, accounting for radiation, diffusion, and convection. The second was to connect what is known about flammability and extinction limits of hot flames with what is known for cool flames. The third was to verify, on the ground, the phenomena seen in the ISS flame extinguishment experiments. The fourth was to collect new quantitative data on extinction limits, flame temperature, and species distributions for validating kinetic and radiation models of cool flames. The fifth was to carry the ISS droplet findings over to a more general ground system that keeps a one-dimensional geometry while including radiation, diffusion, convection, and low-temperature chemistry.

## 3. Experimental Approach

The work combined computation and ground experiments, though the supplied approach text describes only the computational part in detail. Using the conditions of the FLEX flight experiments, the team compared how oxygen concentration affects cool flame extinction limits with three numerical schemes: spark-assisted flame propagation in n-heptane/air mixtures, one-dimensional freely propagating dimethyl ether (DME) flames solved with an arc-length continuation method, and simulations of cool flame regimes and burning limits at elevated pressure, up to 20 atm, in DME/air mixtures.

The ground counterflow experiments use ozone to sensitize the flames, according to the objectives. The supplied text does not describe burner geometry, flow rates, fuels in the experiments, or measurement hardware, apart from the experimental table listing CH2O planar laser-induced fluorescence (PLIF) and thermometry as one dataset. The source points readers to three papers for specifics: "Dynamics of cool flames," "Understanding Cool Flames and Warm Flames," and a study of low-temperature reactivity of large n-alkanes through cool diffusion flame extinction.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The investigation asks what conditions make a hot flame turn into a cool flame, and how the flammable region of cool flames relates to the flammability and extinction limits of hot flames. It hypothesizes that oxygen concentration, dilution, pressure, and the transport processes of diffusion and convection all influence the cool flame transition.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

**Experimentally observed findings.** The supplied text does not state experimental outcomes in words. The data table implies that extinction limits with and without ozone and flame measurements were produced, but values are not given.

**Computational findings.** The source reports from the calculations that the warm flame has a double reaction zone structure and plays a critical role in the transition between cool and hot flames. At elevated pressure, the flammable regions of warm and cool flames were found to expand, which the source attributes to low-temperature and intermediate-temperature reactivity rising with pressure.

**Scientific interpretation.** The source reasons that, because many real engines run at 20 to 40 atm, cool and warm flames matter for high-pressure near-limit combustion. This is an interpretation tied to engine conditions, not a measurement.

**Potential applications.** The calculations are expected to improve understanding of near-limit flame dynamics and to guide experimental searches for the different flame regimes.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

The project sits under spacecraft fire safety, and its links are to flame behavior, extinction, and flammability limits. Knowing when a flame transitions between hot, warm, and cool modes, and under which oxygen, dilution, and pressure conditions it extinguishes, bears directly on how fires behave near their limits. The source notes that warm flames are especially relevant where residence times are long, as in microgravity.

Prevention, detection, and suppression systems are not discussed in the supplied text. The connection to ISS flame extinguishment data is stated, but no specific spacecraft fire safety guidance is given.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

**Warm flames are a distinct regime.** The finding of a double reaction zone gives warm flames their own structure, separate from hot and cool flames, and the source calls it critical for the transition between the two. This is a computational result.

**Pressure widens the flammable window.** Higher pressure expands the flammable region of cool and warm flames through stronger low and intermediate temperature reactivity. It matters because it shifts where fires can persist.

**Extinction needs more than one limit.** Radiation and stretch can each end a flame, and the project's aim of one diagram covering both reflects that.

**Ozone is a tool, not the subject.** Ozone sensitization lets cool flames be studied on the ground in a controlled one-dimensional setting, helping to reproduce ISS observations.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

The investigation was ground-based, but it was designed to verify ISS flame extinguishment phenomena. The source links microgravity to long residence times, which favor warm flame behavior. It does not discuss specific vehicles or habitats, so applications to lunar or Mars systems would be extrapolation.
