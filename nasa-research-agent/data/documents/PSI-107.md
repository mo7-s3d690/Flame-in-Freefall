# Smoke Point In Co-flow Experiment (SPICE)

SPICE asks a deceptively simple question: how much fuel can a small laminar jet flame burn before it starts releasing soot? That threshold, the *laminar smoke point*, is a standard measure of how strongly a fuel tends to form soot. On Earth, buoyancy stretches and accelerates the flame, which tangles the effects of flow, residence time and chemistry together. By burning co-flowing jet diffusion flames in the microgravity of the International Space Station, SPICE removes most of that buoyant transport and lets the smoke point be studied in a nearly non-buoyant setting. The soot behaviour of these flames also bears on spacecraft fire safety.

## 2. Research Objectives

The central aim is to understand combustion better by observing flames in microgravity, supported by analogous ground experiments. The stated specific objectives are to:

1. Identify the laminar smoke-point properties of non-buoyant jet diffusion flames under various co-flow conditions in microgravity.
2. Determine the effects of burner diameter and co-flow velocity on luminous flame lengths and smoke points.
3. Quantify the sooting propensity of different fuels, including ethylene, propane and propylene mixtures.
4. Validate the correlation between luminous flame shape and residence time under microgravity conditions.
5. Investigate how variations in air and fuel flow rates affect smoke-point phenomena in space.

Together these address a gap: smoke-point and flame-length correlations are well established in normal gravity, but their behaviour when buoyancy is removed is much less constrained.

## 3. Experimental Approach

**Setup.** The experiment ran in the Microgravity Science Glovebox on the ISS. The apparatus combined a flow duct with integrated nozzles, cameras and a fuel delivery system. Each flame was ignited in a controlled co-flow air environment.

**Fuels.** Methane, ethylene, propane, propylene, and propylene mixtures. The experimental table (previewed rows) reports fuel flow in standard cubic centimetres per minute (sccm), which suggests gaseous fuel supply; the supplied text does not label the fuel phase explicitly.

**Conditions and variables.**

- *Independent variables:* fuel type; burner (nozzle) diameter of 0.41 mm, 0.76 mm or 1.6 mm; co-flow air velocity from 5.4 cm/s to 65 cm/s; fuel flow rate.
- *Procedure:* fuel flow rates were adjusted to find the smoke point, marked by the flame's transition to emitting soot.
- *Dependent variables / measurements:* flame height (luminous flame length), radiative heat losses, and co-flow air velocity distributions.
- *Scale:* 526 flames were studied in total.

**Instruments.** Still and video cameras recorded flame lengths. The supplied text does not give camera, sensor or radiometer specifications.

**Data table.** Each record in the experimental table lists GMT date, ignition time, flame-off time, fuel, nozzle diameter (mm), fuel rate (sccm and mg/s), hydrocarbon fuel rate (mg/s) and air rate (cm/s). The five rows previewed are all ethylene in the 1.6002 mm nozzle, with fuel rates of about 51 to 138 sccm (0.968 to 2.62 mg/s) and air rates of 36.39 to 57.08 cm/s. Two of the preview rows appear identical, which may be a duplicate or an artifact of the preview. The table is listed as having 470 entries, which differs from the 526 flames stated in the Approach text (see Section 10).

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The stated hypothesis is that, by minimizing gravity-driven transport, the laminar smoke-point properties of non-buoyant jet diffusion flames can be studied directly, and that flame properties will differ significantly from those observed in normal gravity. This is an expectation stated in the record, not a result.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

The record places SPICE under Fire Safety and links it to soot formation and safer spacecraft environments. Justified connections:

- **Flame behaviour:** luminous flame length and shape in microgravity, and their relation to residence time, are basic descriptors of how a diffusion flame behaves without buoyancy.
- **Soot and smoke:** the smoke point marks where a flame begins to emit soot. Soot production matters for smoke generation, radiative heat loss and the hazards of combustion products in a closed cabin.
- **Combustion modeling:** the record states the data will help refine computational models for flame behaviour under non-buoyant conditions.
- **Fuel comparison:** quantifying the sooting propensity of different fuels provides a comparative basis for how fuel chemistry affects soot output.

The supplied material does not address ignition and extinction limits, oxygen concentration, suppression or material flammability, so no claims are made about them.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

1. **Smoke point as a controlled sooting metric.** Using the flame's transition to soot emission as an endpoint gives a repeatable measure across fuels. *Status: methodological design, not a finding.*
2. **Geometry and flow as controlling parameters.** Burner diameter (0.41 to 1.6 mm) and co-flow velocity (5.4 to 65 cm/s) were varied systematically to test their influence on flame length and smoke point. *Status: experimental design; outcomes not supplied.*
3. **Residence time and flame shape.** The investigation targets validating a correlation between luminous flame shape and residence time in microgravity. This matters because soot formation and burnout depend on how long gases spend in the flame. *Status: stated objective.*
4. **Radiative heat loss.** Measuring radiative heat loss alongside flame height links soot to the flame's energy balance. *Status: measurement included; results not supplied.*
5. **Dataset size.** 526 flames provide a sizeable microgravity dataset, which supports model validation. *Status: stated in the record; the table preview reports 470 entries.*

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

The record states that SPICE will improve understanding of soot formation and help design safer spacecraft environments. The data could inform soot and smoke behaviour in microgravity for spacecraft and the ISS, and by extension for future pressurized habitats and exploration vehicles, once validated models are available. The supplied material does not mention lunar or Mars habitats specifically, and nothing indicates these applications have been implemented. They are potential implications only.

## 9. Earth Benefits

The record cites two Earth-side benefits: a better understanding of soot generation, and possible insights toward better fuel formulations and combustion processes (hedged in the source with "could possibly"). Refined computational models for predicting flame behaviour may also have terrestrial uses. No specific industrial or transportation application is described in the supplied material.
