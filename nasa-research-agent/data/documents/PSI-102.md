# Smoke and Aerosol Measurement Experiment (SAME)

When a material overheats and smolders on a spacecraft, the smoke it releases does not behave the way it does on Earth. Without buoyancy to carry hot gas upward, vapor lingers near the source, and the droplets that form there have more time to grow. SAME set out to measure how large those smoke particles actually become in low gravity, and to find out whether the smoke detectors flown on spacecraft still respond properly to them.

## 2. Research Objectives

The main goal was to quantify the sizes reached by liquid smoke particulate in low gravity and to connect the droplet size distribution to the conditions under which smoke was generated and transported in the experiment. A second goal was computational: researchers planned to develop a numerical code that predicts smoke droplet growth from three inputs, namely the fuel pyrolysis rate, the thermodynamic properties of the pyrolysis vapor, and the flow environment.

The underlying problem is practical. Spacecraft smoke detectors were designed and qualified with the behavior of smoke in normal gravity in mind. If low-gravity smoke has a different size distribution, the detectors' sensitivity could differ in ways nobody had measured.

## 3. Experimental Approach

This was a physical flight experiment with a ground-based comparison component. The setup and procedure described in the source were as follows.

The crew installed the near field hardware in the Glovebox, attached the Far Field Box to the Glovebox, and positioned video cameras. The operator ran the self-diagnostic procedures on the two smoke detectors, started the cameras, switched on an igniter for a predefined period of 15 to 60 seconds, and started the thermophoretic soot samplers to collect smoke. Each combustion event typically lasted about 2 minutes.

After the flight, particulate collected on the thermophoretic probes was examined by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) to determine primary particle and aggregate dimensions. The remains of the samples were weighed to obtain mass loss. Detector responses were obtained by transcribing digital data from the video record. Once flight mass loss rates were known, the team worked out which 1-g operating conditions would produce the same mass loss, which allowed a like-for-like comparison between gravity levels.

The smoke came from overheated spacecraft materials, with sample temperature, flow field, and particle aging time controlled more rigorously than in a typical fire test.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The investigation was motivated by three hypotheses, though the supplied text spells out only one in full. It states that in microgravity, with no strong buoyancy-driven flow, smoke from a pyrolyzing or smoldering source grows through nucleation, condensation, and coagulation into larger particles than in 1g. The reasoning is that reduced velocities and mixing raise vapor concentration and residence time near the source. In particular, liquid aerosol smokes were expected to have substantially larger droplets in microgravity under otherwise identical conditions, with a matching drop in particle number density for the same mass of smoke.

The stated consequence is that this shift in size distribution and number density would change how smoke detectors respond, and some detectors might fail to alarm. The other two hypotheses are not itemized in the supplied material.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

The investigation is directly about fire detection. Smoke detectors rely on how particles scatter light or carry charge, and both depend on particle size and number. If microgravity shifts the size distribution, detector response could shift with it. SAME was designed to measure that effect rather than assume it.

The source also places the work inside the Fire Prevention, Detection, and Suppression (FPDS) program plan, which allows future sensor technology to be re-evaluated as it matures. SAME data would give that process a low-gravity reference point. The hypothesis also touches smoke transport, since flow conditions and aging time were controlled variables.

Material flammability, ignition, extinction, and oxygen effects are not addressed in the supplied text, so no claims are made about them.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

**Gravity may change the smoke itself, not just where it goes.** The central idea is that buoyancy removal alters growth conditions near the source, which in turn alters particle size. This is a hypothesis in the supplied material and not a reported result.

**Detector adequacy depends on measured particle properties.** The investigation links particle size distribution to detector response and raises the possibility of missed alarms. Whether any detector actually failed is not stated.

**Comparing flight and ground requires matched mass loss.** The team determined 1-g conditions that reproduced the flight mass loss rate. This design choice makes size differences attributable to gravity rather than to differing amounts of smoke.

**Controlled variables isolate smoke behavior.** Fixing temperature, flow, and aging time lets the experiment separate formation effects from transport effects. The test matrix includes aging durations of 1 and 20 minutes, which suggests particle evolution over time was examined.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

The source frames SAME as supporting advanced fire detection for future long-duration crewed missions. The experiment itself ran on the ISS, and the publication list includes a model of smoke detection in the ISS Destiny Laboratory. The data are intended to let designers judge whether existing detection technology is adequate for low-gravity smoke, and to serve as a baseline for newer sensors.

Lunar habitats, Mars habitats, and pressurized rovers are not mentioned in the supplied text. Any extension to those environments would be an inference, particularly since partial-gravity smoke behavior was not part of the described work.

## 9. Earth Benefits

The source states that detectors developed from SAME results could be useful in other extreme environments on Earth, such as submarines and underwater laboratories, where accurate smoke detection can save lives. No other terrestrial uses are claimed in the supplied material.
