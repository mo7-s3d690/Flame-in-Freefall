# Structure and Liftoff In Combustion Experiment (SLICE)

A candle flame on Earth is deceptively simple. Its familiar teardrop shape is not a property of the combustion chemistry — it is a product of gravity pulling cool, dense air downward and pushing buoyant hot gases upward, stretching the flame into the form we recognize. Remove gravity, and that organizing force disappears entirely. What remains is the combustion chemistry operating in a much more symmetric, diffusion-dominated environment where the structure of the flame — particularly the region at its base where fuel and oxidizer first meet — becomes accessible to study in ways that Earth-based experiments cannot achieve. The SLICE investigation exploited precisely this opportunity, using the ISS to examine the structure and stability of laminar diffusion flames with a level of physical clarity that only the microgravity environment allows.

## 2. Research Objectives

Laminar diffusion flames — in which fuel and oxidizer mix at the flame front rather than being premixed before ignition — are the fundamental combustion mode of candles, gas jets, and many practical burners. Despite their apparent simplicity, the physics governing their base region, where the flame attaches to the burner and transitions from unburned fuel to active combustion, involves a complex interplay of fluid dynamics, chemical kinetics, thermal diffusion, and radiation. On Earth, buoyancy constantly distorts this region, making it difficult to isolate individual governing factors.

SLICE targeted five specific questions:

1. What mechanisms stabilize a laminar diffusion flame at its base in the absence of buoyancy — and how does that stabilization differ from what is observed in normal gravity?
2. What are the **liftoff velocity limits** for different fuels and burner diameters? Liftoff occurs when the coflowing air velocity exceeds the speed at which the flame can anchor itself, causing the flame base to detach from the burner rim and move downstream.
3. How does **nitrogen dilution of the fuel** affect soot formation and overall flame stability?
4. How do microgravity conditions alter **flame length** and **radiative heat loss** compared to identical flames burned in normal gravity?
5. Can numerical combustion models for flame dynamics, soot production, and thermal radiation in non-premixed flames be validated against the clean microgravity experimental data?

## 3. Experimental Approach

### Apparatus Design

The SLICE hardware consisted of a **fan-driven coflow duct** with exchangeable burner tubes of different diameters — a configuration that allowed the coflow air velocity and the burner geometry to be varied independently. Fuel was supplied through the central burner tube into the coflowing air stream, creating a non-premixed (diffusion) flame at the burner exit. Integrated sensors monitored flow rates and temperature, and their data was overlaid directly onto the high-resolution image data for synchronized analysis.

### Fuel Conditions

The investigation tested three main fuel types across the 13 experimental sessions:

- **100% methane (CH₄)** — the simplest hydrocarbon fuel, well-characterized in combustion models and free of soot at lean conditions.
- **Nitrogen-diluted methane** — mixtures of 40% CH₄ / 60% N₂ and 70% CH₄ / 30% N₂, allowing the effect of fuel dilution on flame structure and soot to be isolated from fuel type effects.
- **Nitrogen-diluted ethylene (C₂H₄)** — tested at 20% C₂H₄ / 80% N₂; ethylene produces substantially more soot than methane and is a standard test fuel for soot formation research.

The partial experimental table shows sessions conducted in February 2012, covering pure methane, diluted methane at multiple concentrations, and diluted ethylene. Calibration tests for airflow velocity, soot measurement, and fan performance were conducted within the same test periods to ensure data accuracy.

### Imaging and Data Collection

Flame imaging was performed with a **high-resolution camera** capturing still images throughout each test. More than **4,000 images** were acquired across the 13 sessions. These images formed the primary observational record for characterizing flame shape, length, luminosity (indicative of soot), and base structure. Pyrometry calibration tests were included within some sessions to relate image intensity data to temperature information.

Data analysis focused on correlating flame structure and stability with flow dynamics and fuel properties, integrating the sensor data with the image record to build a coherent picture of how the flames responded to changes in conditions.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The central hypothesis of SLICE is concisely stated: **the structure and stability of laminar diffusion flames in microgravity are significantly influenced by burner diameter, coflow velocity, and fuel composition — and are fundamentally different from flames observed in normal gravity.** 

The hypothesis does not simply assert that microgravity flames are different; it identifies the specific variables through which that difference is expected to manifest. Burner diameter determines the fuel jet velocity profile at the flame base; coflow velocity sets the local strain rate and oxygen supply rate at the flame boundary; fuel composition governs both the chemistry and the sooting tendency of the mixture. In normal gravity, buoyancy modifies the effect of all three variables by imposing an additional upward acceleration on the hot gas column. The SLICE hypothesis is that isolating these variables from buoyancy will reveal their individual contributions more clearly than any ground-based experiment can.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

> **Note:** The PSI record does not include a results summary or compiled dataset. The experimental table shows 5 of 13 test sessions at a metadata level (fuel type and date only). Contributions described here are drawn from the five associated publications listed in the PSI record, which represent the primary vehicle through which SLICE results have been communicated. No external results have been introduced beyond what the PSI record and cited publication titles indicate.

### Flame Structure and Gravity Effects

Associated publications document computational and experimental analysis of coflow methane/air diffusion flames under both microgravity and normal gravity conditions. The combined studies examined how fuel dilution, inlet velocity, and gravity level interact to determine flame shape, the distribution of combustion intermediates, and heat release. The removal of buoyancy in microgravity was found to substantially alter flame length and structure — effects that the coupled computational models were required to reproduce.

### CH\* Concentration as a Heat Release Marker

One publication specifically analyzed **CH\* (excited methylidyne radical) concentration** and its relationship to the **heat release rate** in laminar coflow diffusion flames under both gravity conditions. CH\* chemiluminescence is used as an optical proxy for the local heat release rate in flames, and SLICE data allowed this relationship to be characterized in the absence of buoyancy-induced flow distortion, providing a cleaner calibration for this diagnostic technique.

### Soot Formation in Microgravity

Soot production in microgravity flames was a recurring theme across multiple SLICE publications. By comparing soot formation between normal and microgravity conditions — particularly for ethylene flames, which soot heavily — the investigation revealed how buoyancy affects the residence time of soot precursors in the high-temperature zone, and therefore how much soot accumulates. The microgravity environment provides longer residence times in the flame zone (because buoyant acceleration is absent), which influences both soot volume fraction and particle size distribution in ways that numerical models needed to capture.

### Numerical Model Validation

SLICE data served as a benchmark for validating detailed computational combustion models covering flame dynamics, soot production, and thermal radiation in non-premixed flames. The well-controlled microgravity environment — with no buoyancy to introduce asymmetric flow distortions — provided cleaner experimental boundary conditions than ground-based data, making the validation exercise more discriminating. Models that agree with SLICE data under idealized conditions can be extended to normal-gravity configurations with greater confidence in their physical fidelity.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

**Category: fundamental**

SLICE is a fundamental combustion science investigation. Its connections to fire safety are physical and mechanistic rather than directly operational — it generates the kind of knowledge that underpins reliable fire behavior prediction and safe combustion system design.

- **Flame stability and extinction thresholds:** The liftoff velocity limits measured in SLICE characterize the conditions under which a diffusion flame detaches from its anchor point. This is directly analogous to the conditions under which a flame may stabilize or extinguish in a ventilated spacecraft compartment — where airflow from the environmental control system creates a coflow condition around any burning material.
- **Soot characterization for detection purposes:** Soot particles are the primary target of optical and photoelectric smoke detectors. Understanding how soot forms and accumulates under microgravity conditions — including the particle sizes and concentrations produced — is relevant to selecting and calibrating fire detection instruments for spacecraft environments.
- **Radiation heat loss quantification:** Radiative heat loss from flames in microgravity governs both the flame's ability to sustain itself and the heat flux delivered to surrounding materials. Quantifying this in SLICE contributes to the physical models used to predict fire growth and detector response times in spacecraft fire safety analysis.
- **Model validation for spacecraft fire tools:** Numerical models validated against SLICE data are more reliable tools for predicting flame behavior in spacecraft scenarios — including scenarios involving the gaseous fuels (methane, ethylene analogues) that may be present as propellants, coolants, or combustion products in spacecraft systems.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

### Buoyancy Masks the True Stabilization Mechanism
In normal gravity, the upward acceleration of hot combustion gases continuously reshapes the base of a diffusion flame, making it difficult to isolate the chemical and fluid dynamic mechanisms that actually anchor the flame to the burner. SLICE's microgravity environment removed this masking effect, allowing the stabilization mechanisms at the flame base to be studied in their most fundamental form — an advance with implications for understanding flame attachment in any low-flow combustion environment.

### Liftoff Is a Gravity-Sensitive Transition
The velocity at which a diffusion flame lifts off from its burner depends on the balance between the flame's ability to propagate toward the burner rim and the coflow's tendency to push it downstream. Because buoyancy contributes to that balance on Earth, the liftoff threshold changes measurably in microgravity. SLICE data provides the first systematic characterization of this shift for multiple fuels and burner diameters — information that matters for predicting flame behavior in spacecraft ventilation flows.

### Nitrogen Dilution Decouples Soot and Stability Effects
By testing methane at multiple dilution levels and comparing to pure methane, SLICE created a controlled gradient in both flame temperature and soot precursor concentration. This design made it possible to separate the effects of thermal dilution (lower flame temperature) from chemical dilution (fewer carbon-containing species) on soot formation — a distinction that is harder to achieve cleanly in ground experiments where buoyancy introduces additional variables.

### Microgravity Provides the Cleanest Validation Environment for Combustion Models
The well-defined, quasi-symmetric flow environment in microgravity imposes stricter constraints on what a computational model must reproduce. A model that passes validation in the simpler microgravity case — where buoyancy is absent and boundary conditions are cleaner — has demonstrated physical fidelity at a more fundamental level than one validated only against normal-gravity data.

### Soot Residence Time Is Gravity-Dependent
Longer gas residence times in the flame zone (due to the absence of buoyant acceleration) affect soot particle growth and oxidation. SLICE's comparative soot data between normal and microgravity conditions provides direct evidence for this residence-time effect, contributing to a physically grounded understanding of why soot levels differ between the two environments.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

### Predicting Flame Behavior in Spacecraft Ventilation Flows
Every pressurized spacecraft cabin has an environmental control and life support system that circulates air through the cabin. This circulation creates a background coflow around any combustion event. The SLICE liftoff and stability data — characterizing how flames respond to different coflow velocities — is directly applicable to predicting whether a flame ignited in a spacecraft cabin would stabilize, grow, or be blown out by the ventilation flow under a given set of conditions.

### Fire Growth Modeling for Spacecraft Design
The validated numerical models produced from SLICE are tools that spacecraft fire safety engineers can apply to predict fire growth scenarios, detector activation timing, and suppression agent requirements in vehicle designs that have not yet been built. This is the principal path by which fundamental combustion research translates into spacecraft safety margins.

### Soot Loading and Detection System Design
Knowing the soot particle size distributions and concentrations produced by microgravity diffusion flames — both for clean-burning and heavily sooting fuels — informs the selection of fire detection technologies for spacecraft. Optical detectors, ionization detectors, and laser-scatter instruments each have different sensitivities to particle size and concentration, and SLICE data provides the aerosol characterization needed to match the right detector to the expected smoke composition.

### Fuel Dilution as a Fire Risk Mitigation Strategy
The SLICE data on nitrogen-diluted fuel flames demonstrates how reducing fuel concentration in a mixture affects flame stability and soot production. In spacecraft design, the concept of using diluent gases to reduce fire risk in fuel-carrying systems or in cabin atmospheres has direct relevance — and SLICE provides experimental backing for how much dilution is needed to meaningfully change flame behavior in microgravity.

## 9. Earth Benefits

### Cleaner Industrial Combustion Systems
Soot from diffusion flames is a primary contributor to particulate emissions from gas turbines, industrial burners, and flares. The insights from SLICE into how soot formation responds to fuel dilution, residence time, and coflow conditions — derived in a buoyancy-free environment that isolates these factors clearly — are applicable to the design of low-emission combustion chambers on Earth, where the same chemical mechanisms operate even if the flow environment is more complex.

### Improved Combustion Simulation Tools
The validated numerical models from SLICE improve the accuracy of computational fluid dynamics tools used in commercial combustion system design. Engine manufacturers, power generation companies, and industrial burner designers all use these tools — and models with better-validated soot and radiation physics produce more reliable emissions and efficiency predictions.

### Advanced Engine Development
Understanding flame liftoff behavior in controlled conditions informs combustion chamber design for applications where flame stability at the burner is a critical engineering constraint — gas turbine combustors, hydrogen burners, and advanced natural gas systems among them. The SLICE liftoff velocity limits and their fuel-type dependence contribute reference data for this engineering domain.

### Foundational Radiation Physics for Fire Modeling
The quantification of radiative heat loss from microgravity flames — where radiation is not masked by dominant convective heat transfer — provides clean physical data for the radiation sub-models used in building and industrial fire simulation software. More accurate radiation models lead to better predictions of fire spread, detector activation, and structural heating in terrestrial fire scenarios.

## 10. Limitations and Open Questions

- **No compiled results summary is included in the PSI record.** The experimental table shows 5 of 13 sessions at a date-and-fuel-type level only. Quantitative flame measurements — lengths, liftoff velocities, soot volume fractions, radiative fractions — are contained in the associated publications rather than the PSI entry.
- **No hardware identifier is listed.** The PSI record does not name the specific hardware platform or containment facility used for SLICE on the ISS. This omission limits the ability to contextualize the experiments within the broader ISS research infrastructure.
- **No investigation start or end date is provided** in the PSI record, making it impossible to determine the total active experimental period from the supplied material alone.
- **No associated modeling table.** Despite numerical model validation being a stated objective, no companion computational dataset is associated with the PSI entry.
- **Limited fuel range.** The 13 sessions cover methane, nitrogen-diluted methane, and nitrogen-diluted ethylene. Pure ethylene, propane, hydrogen, and liquid fuels are absent from the tested conditions, leaving the generalizability of the liftoff and soot results to other fuel types as an open question.
- **Liftoff velocity limits not quantified in the supplied material.** Specific liftoff threshold values for each fuel and burner diameter combination are not reported in the PSI record and require access to the full publications.
- **Transition to turbulent flames not addressed.** SLICE focused exclusively on laminar flames. How the microgravity effects on structure and stability extend into the transitional and turbulent regimes — which are more relevant to larger fires — is outside the investigation's scope.
