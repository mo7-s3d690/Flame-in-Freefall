# Smoke Aerosol Measurement Experiment — Reflight (SAME-R)

Detecting a fire in space before it grows beyond control depends entirely on sensors responding to what a fire actually produces in that environment. On Earth, smoke detectors are calibrated against smoke particles generated under gravity — particles whose sizes, concentrations, and physical forms are all shaped by the buoyant flows that carry them away from the source. In microgravity, those flows are absent, and the particles that form near a smoldering or pyrolyzing surface behave differently from the moment of their creation. They stay near the source longer, have more time to grow through condensation and coagulation, and may reach sizes that cause standard detection technologies to respond differently — or not at all. SAME-R was the second attempt to measure those particles directly in orbit, correcting the contamination problems that compromised the first investigation's physical samples and extending the range of conditions and instruments used to characterize microgravity smoke.

## 2. Research Objectives

The original SAME investigation (PSI-102) returned physically contaminated thermal precipitator samples — the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) grids intended to capture smoke particles for post-flight morphological analysis had been fouled by unidentified foreign material during the mission, rendering most grids unusable. The science objective survived the hardware failure because other instruments continued to gather data, but the most direct physical characterization of particle morphology was lost. SAME-R was designed to recover that capability while simultaneously broadening the experimental scope.

The primary scientific goal remained consistent with SAME: to **quantify the particle size distributions of liquid smoke aerosols generated in microgravity** and to relate those distributions to the conditions under which the smoke was generated and transported. Knowing particle size matters because every smoke detection technology — photoelectric scattering, ionization, light extinction, condensation counting — has a sensitivity curve that varies with particle size and number density. If microgravity smoke systematically shifts that distribution toward larger particles and lower number densities compared to normal-gravity smoke from the same source, existing spacecraft detectors may be calibrated for a particle population they will rarely encounter in service.

Specific improvements targeted in the reflight included:

- Replacing the original fuel sample material with one that had not caused contamination issues.
- Extending the range of heating temperatures and ventilation conditions tested.
- Introducing a new in-house instrument (M-pass, developed at GRC) to supplement the commercial diagnostics inherited from SAME.
- Generating a cleaner dataset for validating the lognormal distribution assumption and the moment method used to extract particle size statistics from the raw detector signals.

## 3. Experimental Approach

### Hardware Inheritance and Modification

SAME-R used the same on-orbit hardware platform as the original SAME investigation, minimizing development costs and preserving direct comparability with SAME results. Three targeted modifications were made to address the specific problems encountered in the original flight:

**1. New fuel material — Pyrell® foam:** The original SAME investigation used dibutyl phthalate (DBP) samples as a smoke source. SAME-R replaced these with **Pyrell®**, an intumescent fire-retardant foam manufactured by Foamex. Intumescent materials swell and char when heated, producing smoke in a way that is more representative of real spacecraft materials in a fire scenario. The substitution also eliminated the contamination source that had fouled the SAME TEM grids.

**2. Broadened test conditions:** Heating temperatures were extended to higher values than used in SAME, and a no-flow condition during sample heating was added. These changes allowed the investigation to characterize smoke generation and particle growth across a wider range of source conditions, including scenarios where the absence of forced ventilation during the smoldering phase would be expected to maximize particle residence time near the source.

**3. New diagnostic instrument — M-pass:** One of the two Commercial Diagnostic Enclosures (CDEs) from SAME — each containing a DustTrak particle counter and an ionization detector — was replaced with the **M-pass**, an instrument developed in-house at GRC. The M-pass provided an independent measurement channel with different detection characteristics, expanding the diagnostic coverage of the instrument suite.

### Test Materials and Conditions

The primary fuel material was **Kapton** (polyimide film), which appears throughout the experimental table, with Pyrell® as the principal new addition. Kapton is widely used in spacecraft as insulation for wiring and flexible circuits, making it directly relevant as a fire scenario material. Test conditions varied across heating temperature, inlet airflow velocity, and the timing of flow relative to sample heating — the experimental table shows inlet velocities of 8.1 cm/s and characterizes tests by ratios of thermal precipitator resistance states, smoke aging time, mixing time, and net aging time.

### Measurement Methodology

The core measurement technique relied on the **moment method** — a mathematical approach to extracting particle size distribution parameters (mean size and width of the distribution) from the aggregate response of multiple sensors rather than from direct imaging of individual particles. The method assumes that the aerosol follows a **lognormal size distribution**, an assumption validated by both the SAME post-flight analysis and the three ground-based validation tests conducted after the SAME-R flight campaign.

Thermal precipitators collected physical smoke samples on TEM grids for post-flight morphological analysis — the same approach as SAME, but with Pyrell® as the source material and improved sample handling to avoid the contamination that compromised the original. Optical and ionization detectors provided continuous in-flight data throughout each 60–80 second test point.

### Post-Flight Validation

Three ground test campaigns followed the flight experiment to validate the data reduction pipeline and confirm that the lognormal assumption was appropriate across the range of conditions tested in SAME-R. This post-flight validation step reflects the methodological care required when the measurement approach relies on a statistical model of the underlying particle population rather than direct particle-by-particle characterization.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The hypothesis driving SAME-R is inherited directly from the original SAME investigation, and its logic is grounded in the physics of aerosol growth. In microgravity, the absence of buoyancy-driven convection means that smoke particles generated near a smoldering surface are not rapidly carried away into a large mixing volume. Instead, they remain in the high-vapor-concentration region near the source for longer, where nucleation, condensation onto existing particles, and particle-particle coagulation can proceed for extended periods before dilution limits further growth.

The specific prediction is that **liquid aerosol smoke particles produced in microgravity will be substantially larger than particles produced under identical conditions in normal gravity**, with a correspondingly lower particle number density for the same mass of smoke generated. The size shift is not expected to be marginal — the hypothesis anticipates a qualitatively different particle population, not merely a statistical perturbation of the ground-based distribution.

The fire safety implication of this hypothesis is explicit: a systematic shift toward larger, less numerous particles may cause photoelectric and ionization detectors — both calibrated against normal-gravity smoke — to generate weaker signals or fail to reach alarm threshold concentrations when exposed to realistic microgravity smoke at dangerous fire growth stages. The investigation was designed to test whether this failure mode is real and to quantify its magnitude.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

> **Note:** The PSI record provided does not include a compiled results summary. The experimental table shows 5 of 110 test entries at a metadata level, with instrument configuration and timing parameters but no particle size or detector response values. Contributions described here are based on the investigation's objectives, approach, the modifications made relative to SAME, and the titles and authors of the six associated publications. No results have been fabricated.

### Recovery of Physical Sample Analysis

By replacing the contamination-prone DBP source material with Pyrell® and improving sample handling procedures, SAME-R recovered the capability to analyze smoke particle morphology from physically collected TEM samples — the measurement category that SAME had been unable to deliver. This restoration of the physical sample record provides particle shape information and direct size confirmation that complements the continuous sensor data.

### Extended Parameter Coverage

The broadened test matrix — higher heating temperatures, no-flow heating conditions, and an additional instrument channel — produced a dataset that covers a wider region of the smoke generation parameter space than SAME alone. This coverage is necessary for generalizing the particle size findings beyond the narrow conditions of the original investigation and for establishing how sensitively particle growth depends on source temperature and airflow during the smoldering phase.

### Detector Performance Characterization

Associated publications document the evaluation of spacecraft smoke detector performance against low-gravity smoke across the SAME and SAME-R combined dataset. This represents the direct translation of the aerosol measurement data into fire safety terms: for each detector type, what particle sizes and concentrations are required to trigger an alarm, and how does that threshold compare to what SAME-R found microgravity smoke to actually contain?

### Lognormal Distribution Validation

The three post-flight ground validation campaigns confirmed the applicability of the lognormal size distribution model and the moment method to the SAME-R data. This methodological validation is not merely procedural — it establishes the statistical foundation on which the entire particle size characterization rests, and makes the SAME-R results interpretable in terms that can be used in detector design specifications.

### Comparison with SAME Results

By maintaining hardware continuity with the original SAME investigation while modifying materials, conditions, and instruments, SAME-R generated results that can be directly compared to the SAME dataset. This comparison is necessary for assessing the reproducibility and generalizability of the microgravity smoke characterization, and for understanding how sensitive the particle size findings are to the choice of source material and heating conditions.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

**Category: detection**

SAME-R is focused squarely on the detection problem in spacecraft fire safety. It does not study flames, flammability limits, or suppressants — it studies the aerosol that a fire produces before it is visible, and whether the instruments tasked with detecting that aerosol are actually capable of doing so in the environment where they are deployed.

- **Detector calibration gap:** If microgravity smoke particles are systematically larger and less numerous than normal-gravity particles, detectors designed for the latter will be under-sensitive to the former. SAME-R quantifies this gap, providing the data needed to determine whether recalibration, redesign, or replacement of existing spacecraft smoke detectors is warranted.
- **Early detection before visible flames:** The smoldering and pyrolysis phase that precedes open flaming is precisely the window in which smoke detection systems are most valuable. SAME-R characterizes the aerosol in this pre-flame phase under microgravity conditions, which is when early detection matters most and when the difference between normal-gravity and microgravity aerosol physics is most pronounced.
- **Detector technology selection for future spacecraft:** SAME-R results provide quantitative sensitivity benchmarks against which candidate detection technologies for long-duration exploration missions can be evaluated. Without microgravity-specific aerosol data, technology selection would be based on normal-gravity performance metrics that may not predict spacecraft performance.
- **Ionization and optical detector comparison:** The parallel use of ionization detectors and optical (DustTrak) instruments with the new M-pass channel allows the investigation to characterize how different detection principles respond to the same microgravity smoke — directly answering which detector type is better suited to the actual particle population.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

### Particle Growth Physics Are Gravity-Sensitive at the Source
The fundamental insight underpinning SAME and SAME-R is that particle size is not fixed at the moment smoke is generated — it evolves through condensation and coagulation as the aerosol ages near the source. The rate and extent of that growth depend on vapor concentration and residence time in the high-concentration zone, both of which change substantially when buoyant convective mixing is absent. This is a physical mechanism, not a secondary effect, and it means that microgravity smoke and normal-gravity smoke from the same material are genuinely different aerosols.

### Standard Detector Technologies May Not Be Adequate for Microgravity
The detector performance evaluation published from SAME-R data addresses this concern directly. If the results confirm that existing spacecraft detectors are under-sensitive to larger, less numerous microgravity particles, it establishes a clear technical requirement for next-generation detection systems — not a qualitative concern, but a quantitative gap expressed in terms of particle size and number density thresholds.

### Instrument Development for Microgravity Is a Non-Trivial Engineering Challenge
The contamination failure of the SAME TEM grids, the gravity-dependence of the original P-Trak (addressed by DAFT, PSI-47), and the need to develop the M-pass instrument in-house collectively illustrate that standard laboratory smoke measurement tools cannot simply be transferred to microgravity without systematic review and adaptation. SAME-R embodies this lesson as a design principle.

### Post-Flight Validation Is Essential for Moment-Method Aerosol Analysis
The commitment to three ground-based post-flight validation campaigns reflects the recognition that the moment method's output is only as reliable as the lognormal assumption it depends on. SAME-R demonstrates a standard of methodological rigor appropriate for data that will be used to make engineering decisions about smoke detector specifications.

### Source Material Choice Affects Particle Morphology
The replacement of DBP with Pyrell® as the smoke source was not merely a contamination avoidance decision — it also changed the chemical composition and physical form of the particles being measured. This distinction matters for interpreting the SAME-R results relative to SAME and for understanding how the findings generalize to other spacecraft materials that may smolder in a real fire.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

### Next-Generation Spacecraft Smoke Detectors
The SAME-R dataset provides the aerosol characterization data needed to specify fire detection systems for future spacecraft. Detector manufacturers and NASA engineers can use the particle size distributions, number densities, and detector response data from SAME-R to define minimum sensitivity requirements for ionization, optical, and CNC-type detectors intended for use in Artemis vehicles, the Lunar Gateway, and Mars transit spacecraft.

### Long-Duration Mission Fire Detection
The further a spacecraft travels from Earth, the longer it will take to reach safety if a fire occurs. On a Mars transit mission, early and reliable fire detection is not merely a safety enhancement — it is a prerequisite for mission survivability. SAME-R's characterization of microgravity smoke provides the baseline data against which any fire detection system proposed for such missions must be evaluated.

### Pre-Alarm Smolder Detection
On Earth, smoldering fires in confined spaces — inside electrical equipment, within insulation, or behind structural panels — are notoriously difficult to detect before they grow into open flames. In a spacecraft, the same problem exists but with higher stakes and without the ability to open windows or quickly evacuate. SAME-R's focus on the pre-flame aerosol phase is specifically relevant to this smolder detection challenge.

### Detector Technology Evaluation Framework
The methodology developed in SAME-R — generating a known aerosol under controlled conditions and measuring the response of multiple detector types in parallel — provides a framework for evaluating future detection technologies. Any new sensor proposed for spacecraft use can be evaluated against the same SAME-R aerosol conditions, enabling direct comparison with the existing detector performance benchmarks.

## 9. Earth Benefits

### Confined-Environment Smoke Detection
The same detection gap that SAME-R addresses in spacecraft is relevant in any confined, ventilation-limited environment where smoke particle growth near the source may differ from open-room conditions. Submarines, hyperbaric facilities, underground mines, and aircraft cargo holds all share characteristics — sealed volumes, forced ventilation, limited egress — that make early and reliable smoke detection critical and potentially subject to the same aerosol physics that SAME-R characterized.

### Smoke Detector Standards and Calibration
The quantitative particle size and number density data from SAME-R can inform the development of more physically grounded smoke detector calibration standards on Earth. Currently, detector sensitivity standards use specific aerosol reference conditions that may not capture the range of real-world smoke characteristics — particularly in environments with unusual airflow or temperature conditions. SAME-R demonstrates the value of environment-specific aerosol characterization for meaningful detector calibration.

### Industrial Fire Detection in Low-Ventilation Environments
In enclosed industrial facilities — transformer vaults, battery storage rooms, computer server rooms — the airflow patterns that govern smoke transport and particle growth can differ significantly from the open-room conditions assumed in standard detector certification tests. The SAME-R aerosol physics results contribute to understanding how these environment-specific conditions affect detector performance.

### Advanced Aerosol Instrumentation Development
The M-pass instrument developed in-house at GRC for SAME-R represents a contribution to aerosol measurement technology. In-house development for a specific application — measuring smoke particles accurately in microgravity — typically produces methodological advances applicable more broadly. The instrument and the measurement approaches validated through SAME-R are potentially transferable to other aerosol characterization problems in both space and terrestrial contexts.

## 10. Limitations and Open Questions

- **No compiled results summary is included in the PSI record.** The experimental table shows 5 of 110 test points at a configuration metadata level; particle size values, detector response data, and lognormal fit parameters are in the associated publications.
- **Hardware identifier is non-specific.** The PSI record notes "Comparative Soot Diagnostic Hardware; Refer to final report" without naming specific instruments in a way that allows the hardware configuration to be fully understood from the PSI entry alone.
- **Limited fuel material coverage.** SAME-R primarily used Kapton and Pyrell®. How the particle size findings generalize to smoke from other common spacecraft materials — PEEK, Ultem, fluoropolymer wire insulation, fabric samples — is not addressed within this investigation.
- **No associated modeling table.** Despite the moment method being a mathematical model for extracting size distribution parameters, no computational companion dataset is associated with the PSI-101 record.
- **Short flight window.** The investigation ran for approximately two months (July–September 2010), limiting the number of conditions and materials that could be tested within the available crew time and hardware capacity.
- **Relationship between ground and flight results.** The post-flight ground validation tests were intended to support data reduction rather than to independently generate a parallel flight-equivalent dataset. The degree to which ground conditions accurately replicate the full microgravity aerosol physics — particularly the near-source particle growth in the absence of buoyancy — remains an inherent limitation of any ground validation approach.
- **Transition from smoldering to open flaming not addressed.** SAME-R characterizes aerosols from pyrolyzing and smoldering sources. How the particle population changes if smoldering transitions to open flaming — and what that transition means for detector response — is outside the investigation's scope.
