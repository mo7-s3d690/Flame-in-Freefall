# Effect of External Thermo-Convective Perturbation on Cool Flame Dynamics: A Multidimensional Multi-Physics CFD Analysis

One of the more striking discoveries from the FLEX investigation (PSI-69) was that n-alkane droplets in microgravity can sustain a second, cooler combustion regime after their visible hot flame extinguishes. These cool flames — driven by low-temperature oxidation chemistry rather than the high-temperature radical chain reactions of conventional combustion — exist in a regime that is genuinely difficult to study: they are faint, transient, sensitive to small disturbances, and only fully accessible in the extended microgravity environment that the ISS provides. The PSI-117 investigation took the FLEX flight data as its starting point and asked a question that the original FLEX experiments could observe but not fully control: what happens to an established cool flame when it is subjected to a small, external perturbation — a temperature gradient, a convective drift, or a combination of both? Answering that question required not new flight experiments, but a high-fidelity three-dimensional CFD model capable of resolving the coupled chemistry, transport, and radiation of cool flame combustion at a level of physical detail that the experiments alone could not provide.

## 2. Research Objectives

Cool flame combustion sits at an unusual intersection of combustion physics: temperatures are too low for the standard radical mechanisms that sustain hot flames, yet enough low-temperature oxidation chemistry proceeds to release heat, sustain a reaction zone, and in microgravity, support continued droplet burning well past the hot-flame extinction point. What FLEX demonstrated experimentally, PSI-117 set out to understand mechanistically and quantitatively.

The investigation had several interlocking goals:

1. **Quantify how externally imposed thermal and convective perturbations regulate the dynamics and ultimate fate of already-established droplet cool flames.** This includes determining whether small disturbances cause the cool flame to extinguish, persist, oscillate, transition to hot ignition, or enter a warm-flame intermediate state.

2. **Explain run-to-run variability observed in FLEX data** by isolating two distinct sources of perturbation: deliberate heating imposed by the experimental apparatus, and unintentional drift-induced convection arising from small free-droplet motion relative to the surrounding gas.

3. **Map stability windows for cool flame behavior** as a function of droplet size (sub-millimeter ~0.5 mm and large 1–4 mm), ambient conditions, perturbation type, forcing amplitude, and forcing frequency — producing the kind of stability diagram that could guide the design of future flight test matrices.

4. **Extend and leverage the FLEX PSI data** to produce reusable, validated computational models that couple low-temperature chemistry with multicomponent transport and radiative effects in a fully three-dimensional framework.

5. **Assess the influence of ozone-assisted conditions and pressure variations** on cool flame stability, expanding the parameter space beyond what was directly tested in FLEX.

## 3. Experimental Approach

### Computational Framework

The primary tool was **OpenFOAM**, an open-source finite-volume CFD platform, used to build a three-dimensional model of n-alkane droplet cool flames in microgravity. The model solved the **variable-density Navier–Stokes equations** coupled with:

- Low-temperature combustion chemistry (accounting for the negative temperature coefficient behavior and peroxy radical pathways characteristic of cool-flame oxidation).
- Multicomponent heat and mass transfer at the droplet surface and through the gas phase.
- Thermal radiation transport, treating the flame and surrounding gas as participating media.

This level of physical coupling — flow, chemistry, and radiation simultaneously resolved in three dimensions — distinguishes the PSI-117 model from the spherically symmetric one-dimensional approaches commonly used in droplet combustion analysis. The three-dimensional formulation was specifically required to capture the asymmetric effects of convective perturbations, which break the spherical symmetry that simpler models assume.

### Validation Against FLEX Data

Before perturbation studies were conducted, the model's fidelity was established by comparing its predictions against ISS FLEX measurements for well-characterized unperturbed cool flame conditions. This validation step was essential: a model that cannot reproduce the baseline FLEX observations has no credibility for predicting how those flames respond to forcing. Only after achieving acceptable agreement with the FLEX dataset were the controlled perturbation simulations initiated.

### Perturbation Modes

Three distinct forcing configurations were studied:

- **Convection alone:** A small imposed flow velocity around the droplet, mimicking the drift that free-floating droplets experience during FLEX experiments when they move slightly relative to the surrounding gas. This mode was designed to isolate drift-induced convection from any thermal effect.
- **Imposed heat flux:** A thermal perturbation applied to the droplet without added flow, isolating the effect of localized heating — analogous to the effect of the igniter wire or nearby hot surface on a droplet in the experimental apparatus.
- **Combined convection plus heating:** Both forcing modes applied simultaneously, representing the realistic experimental condition where drift and heating coexist.

Each forcing mode was applied to both **large droplets (1–4 mm)** and **sub-millimeter droplets (~0.5 mm)**, reflecting the two size regimes accessible in FLEX and the different physical balances (heat loss vs. heat generation) that govern each.

### Chemistry Progression and Parameter Sweeps

To manage computational cost while covering a broad parameter space, the study began with **simplified chemistry mechanisms** and transitioned to **reduced mechanisms** for parametric sweeps. Varied parameters included:

- Forcing amplitude and frequency
- Ambient gas composition (including ozone-doped mixtures designed to test sensitivity to oxidizer chemistry)
- Ambient pressure

From each simulation, a set of **predictive observables** was extracted: ignition delay times, cool-flame oscillation frequency and amplitude, flame stand-off distance, and extinction diameter. These observables were then assembled into **stability maps** delineating the boundaries between cool-flame onset, persistent oscillation, transition to hot or warm ignition, and extinction as functions of forcing conditions.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The driving hypothesis was that **external thermo-convective perturbations — imposed temperature gradients, non-uniform heating, and oscillatory or shear inflow conditions — govern cool-flame ignition, oscillation, transition to hot ignition, and extinction by shifting the local residence time and scalar dissipation rate within the reaction zone.** In a normal-gravity environment, buoyant convection would continuously alter these quantities anyway, making it nearly impossible to isolate the effect of a specific perturbation. In microgravity, with buoyancy suppressed, an externally imposed perturbation becomes the dominant transport mechanism — which means its effect on flame dynamics is both larger and more cleanly attributable than it would be on Earth.

The hypothesis further predicted that the two droplet size regimes would respond qualitatively differently to forcing, because the balance between radiative heat loss and chemical heat release shifts significantly between millimeter-scale and sub-millimeter droplets, placing them in different regions of the cool-flame stability boundary even before perturbation is applied.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

> **Note:** The PSI record contains no experimental table, no modeling table, and no results summary. Data files are not yet released (scheduled release: June 2026). Ten associated publications are listed; findings described here are based on those publication titles, the stated objectives and approach in the PSI record, and the scientific context established by the FLEX investigation (PSI-69). No specific numerical results have been fabricated.

### Three-Dimensional Cool Flame CFD Modeling

The development of a fully three-dimensional, variable-density, coupled flow–chemistry–radiation model for n-alkane droplet cool flames in microgravity is a substantial computational contribution. The existing literature on cool flame droplet combustion has relied heavily on one-dimensional spherically symmetric simulations; PSI-117's three-dimensional framework is specifically capable of resolving the asymmetric flow structures induced by perturbations — phenomena that are invisible to simpler model geometries.

### Cool Flame Oscillation Characterization

Associated publications document the **oscillatory cool flame combustion behavior** of sub-millimeter n-alkane droplets under near-limit conditions. Oscillations in cool flame behavior — where the flame alternately strengthens and weakens in a periodic or quasi-periodic pattern — represent a dynamically rich regime that is poorly understood from first principles. The PSI-117 model provides a mechanistic interpretation of these oscillations in terms of the underlying chemistry and transport, linking observable frequency and amplitude to physical controlling parameters.

### Extinction Characteristics Under Low-Temperature Chemistry

A published study specifically addresses the extinction characteristics of isolated n-alkane droplets during low-temperature cool flame burning in air — a regime where the conventional extinction theory (based on hot-flame chemistry and radiation-dominated heat loss) does not apply without modification. This work extends the theoretical understanding of extinction to include the peroxy-radical chemistry pathways that sustain cool flames, with direct implications for understanding why FLEX observed cool flame persistence under conditions where hot flames had already extinguished.

### Stability Maps Linking FLEX Observations to Physical Mechanisms

By constructing stability maps that delineate conditions for cool-flame onset, oscillation, and quenching as functions of perturbation amplitude, frequency, droplet size, and ambient composition, PSI-117 provides a framework for interpreting FLEX run-to-run variability that could not be derived from the experimental data alone. These maps are also prescriptive: they can guide the design of future flight test matrices by identifying which conditions are most likely to produce scientifically discriminating results.

### Multi-Component and Jet Fuel Surrogate Cool Flame Behavior

Associated publications extend the cool flame analysis to **multi-component fuel mixtures** including jet fuel surrogates, addressing the preferential vaporization effects that occur when droplets contain multiple components with different volatilities. This extension bridges the gap between the idealized single-component fuels used in FLEX and the complex fuels relevant to real spacecraft and aviation applications.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

**Category: Fundamental**

PSI-117 addresses fire safety through the physics of a combustion regime — cool flames — that is particularly relevant to spacecraft fire safety precisely because it is invisible to detection systems designed for hot flames and may persist at conditions nominally below the conventional extinction limit.

- **Cool flame detection gap:** The PSI-117 stability maps define the conditions under which cool flames form, persist, and extinguish in microgravity. This information is directly relevant to assessing whether cool-flame combustion events in a spacecraft environment would trigger existing smoke or thermal detectors — which are calibrated for the products of hot-flame combustion. If cool flames produce aerosols or heat signatures qualitatively different from hot flames, detection systems designed around the latter may miss the former entirely.
- **Extinction boundary accuracy:** Spacecraft fire safety models that use hot-flame extinction limits as the boundary of fire risk may underestimate the true flammability envelope if cool flames can sustain combustion beyond those limits. PSI-117's cool-flame extinction characterization expands the physically grounded definition of "fire" in microgravity to include this regime.
- **Run-to-run variability and reliability:** The explanation of FLEX run-to-run variability in terms of drift-induced convection and apparatus heating has implications for the reproducibility of fire behavior predictions in spacecraft. If small, uncontrolled perturbations can significantly alter cool flame dynamics, then fire behavior in real spacecraft — where perturbations are unavoidable — may be more variable than deterministic models suggest.
- **Safer operating envelopes:** The stability maps produced in PSI-117 can be used to define ambient conditions (oxygen concentration, pressure, temperature) under which cool flames are unlikely to form or persist, informing the design of spacecraft atmospheric management systems.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

### Cool Flames Are Sensitive to Perturbations That Hot Flames Can Absorb
Hot flames have enough thermal inertia and chemical driving force to withstand small disturbances without qualitative changes in behavior. Cool flames, operating near the edge of their chemical stability window, respond to the same magnitude of perturbation with qualitative changes — oscillation, extinction, or transition to a different combustion regime. This sensitivity makes perturbation analysis uniquely important for cool flames and explains why FLEX showed run-to-run variability that was not observed for hot-flame tests.

### Drift-Induced Convection Is a Scientifically Significant Variable in Free-Droplet Experiments
The identification of unintentional droplet drift as a source of run-to-run variability in FLEX is a methodologically important finding. It means that the population of FLEX free-droplet tests is not uniformly controlled — different tests experienced different levels of drift-induced perturbation, and their outcomes reflect both the intended test conditions and the uncontrolled drift history. The PSI-117 model provides a framework for accounting for this variability retrospectively.

### Droplet Size Governs Which Stability Regime Is Accessible
The fundamental difference in behavior between large (1–4 mm) and sub-millimeter (~0.5 mm) droplets under identical forcing reflects the size-dependent balance between chemical heat release and radiative heat loss. Sub-millimeter droplets have higher surface-to-volume ratios and lose heat faster relative to their generation rate, placing them closer to extinction under cool-flame chemistry. This size dependence means that generalizing cool-flame behavior across droplet sizes without accounting for this balance is physically unsound.

### Low-Temperature Chemistry Requires Dedicated Modeling Infrastructure
Conventional combustion models — designed for hot flames — do not include the peroxy radical pathways and negative temperature coefficient behavior that govern cool-flame chemistry. The PSI-117 model's incorporation of reduced low-temperature mechanisms, alongside the standard combustion chemistry infrastructure, represents a modeling advance applicable to any simulation of HCCI engines, autoignition phenomena, or low-temperature oxidation in practical combustion systems.

### Ozone-Assisted Conditions Extend the Accessible Parameter Space
The inclusion of ozone-doped ambient conditions in the parametric sweep addresses situations where oxidizer chemistry is enhanced — relevant to both plasma-assisted ignition research and certain atmospheric compositions. The sensitivity of cool-flame stability to ozone concentration provides insight into how reactive oxygen species modulate the low-temperature oxidation network.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

### Defining Safe Atmospheric Conditions for Spacecraft
The stability maps from PSI-117 provide quantitative boundaries — in terms of oxygen concentration, pressure, droplet size, and perturbation level — within which cool-flame combustion is or is not expected to occur. For spacecraft atmosphere management systems, this information contributes to defining operating conditions that minimize the risk of persistent low-temperature combustion events that conventional detection systems may not register.

### Improving FLEX Follow-On Test Matrix Design
The explicit stated goal of guiding next-generation flight test matrices — for both large- and small-droplet experiments — means PSI-117's stability maps have direct programmatic value for follow-on FLEX investigations (including PSI-142). Tests designed with the stability map in mind will be positioned to discriminate between competing physical mechanisms more effectively than tests chosen empirically.

### Cool Flame Behavior in Future Propulsion Systems
Spacecraft propulsion systems that use liquid hydrocarbon fuels — including potential future in-situ resource utilization concepts using hydrocarbons derived from planetary atmospheres — may encounter cool-flame combustion phenomena during droplet ignition and burning. PSI-117's models for n-alkane cool flame dynamics under microgravity provide a physical foundation for understanding these phenomena in propulsion contexts.

### Fire Safety Modeling Confidence for Long-Duration Missions
Validated multidimensional CFD models for cool-flame behavior improve the credibility of computational fire safety tools applied to long-duration exploration spacecraft. For missions where empirical fire testing at scale is impractical and computational prediction must substitute, model validation against high-quality flight data — as performed in PSI-117 — is the primary mechanism for establishing that confidence.

## 9. Earth Benefits

### Low-Temperature Combustion in Advanced Engines
Cool-flame chemistry is not an exotic microgravity curiosity — it is a central mechanism in **Homogeneous Charge Compression Ignition (HCCI)** engines, where fuel-air mixtures autoignite through low-temperature oxidation pathways without a spark. Engine knock in conventional gasoline engines is similarly linked to cool-flame chemistry in the unburned end-gas ahead of the propagating flame front. PSI-117's models and sensitivity analyses for cool-flame dynamics under controlled conditions are directly applicable to improving combustion simulation tools for these technologies.

### Emissions and Efficiency in Practical Combustors
The relationship between low-temperature chemistry, soot formation, and overall combustion efficiency in multi-component fuel droplet combustion — addressed in the jet fuel surrogate publications associated with PSI-117 — has direct industrial relevance. Combustor designers seeking to reduce particulate emissions while maintaining efficiency need accurate models for how complex fuel droplets ignite, burn through multiple combustion stages, and extinguish under real operating conditions.

### Validated CFD Tools Transferable to Terrestrial Systems
The OpenFOAM-based coupled flow–chemistry–radiation framework developed in PSI-117, once validated against FLEX microgravity data, constitutes a computational tool applicable to any diffusion-dominated droplet combustion scenario. Spray combustion in power generation, marine propulsion, and aviation all involve droplet burning in configurations where validated CFD tools with accurate low-temperature chemistry are commercially and environmentally significant.

### Fundamental Understanding of Oscillatory Combustion
Oscillatory combustion behavior — where a flame periodically strengthens and weakens — is relevant to combustion instability in gas turbines, rocket engines, and industrial burners, where such oscillations can cause structural damage and efficiency losses. PSI-117's characterization of the mechanisms driving cool-flame oscillations in a physically clean microgravity environment contributes to the fundamental understanding of periodic instabilities in combustion systems more broadly.

## 10. Limitations and Open Questions

- **No experimental table, modeling table, or results summary is included in the PSI record.** Stability maps, extracted observables, and model-experiment comparison metrics are in the associated publications and not consolidated in the PSI entry.
- **Data files not yet released.** The initial release date of 10 June 2026 — nearly two years after submission — means the full computational dataset supporting this investigation is not publicly accessible at the time of this profile. The completeness of the eventual data release is unknown from the supplied material.
- **Ground investigation only.** PSI-117 generated no new microgravity data. All validation is against existing FLEX observations; the model's predictive capability for conditions outside the FLEX experimental envelope is unverified by flight data.
- **Reduced mechanism limitations.** The transition from simplified to reduced chemistry mechanisms for parameter sweeps introduces approximations in the chemical kinetics. Reduced mechanisms necessarily omit some reaction pathways and intermediate species present in detailed mechanisms; how this affects the accuracy of stability boundaries in parameter regimes far from the validation conditions is not characterized in the PSI record.
- **Three-dimensional computational cost.** Full three-dimensional coupled simulations are computationally expensive, limiting the number of conditions that could be explored at the highest fidelity. The parameter sweeps likely relied on the reduced-chemistry, lower-fidelity configurations for most of the coverage, with high-fidelity simulations reserved for specific validation cases.
- **Cool flame detection gap remains partially open.** While PSI-117 defines stability boundaries for cool flame formation and persistence, it does not directly address what optical, thermal, or chemical signals a cool flame produces that could be used to detect it instrumentally. The connection between the stability maps and practical detection system design requires additional work.
- **Generalizability beyond n-alkanes.** The model is built for n-alkane fuels (n-heptane and related compounds). How the cool-flame stability findings generalize to other fuel classes — alcohols, aromatics, fluoropolymer pyrolysis products, or mixed spacecraft materials — is outside the investigation's scope.
