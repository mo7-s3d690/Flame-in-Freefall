# Spacecraft Fire Experiment-II (SAFFIRE-II)

SAFFIRE-II is a flight experiment that burned solid materials inside an uncrewed Cygnus resupply vehicle after it left the International Space Station. Because the vehicle carried no crew, researchers could ignite samples in a much larger flow volume than any crewed experiment would permit. The goal was to learn how quickly a realistic, large-scale fire grows in low gravity and how the low-gravity flammability limits of spacecraft-relevant materials compare with those from NASA's normal-gravity screening test.

## 2. Research Objectives

The stated objectives are to:

1. Determine how rapidly a large-scale fire grows in low gravity.
2. Investigate low-gravity flammability limits and compare them with those from NASA's normal-gravity material flammability screening test.

Spacecraft fire safety has relied on terrestrial fire behavior and standards, even though flames behave very differently in reduced gravity. SAFFIRE-II was meant to supply data that spacecraft designers currently lack.

## 3. Experimental Approach

**Platform.** The experiment ran on the Cygnus vehicle after it deberthed from the ISS, so it was a true orbital microgravity test rather than a ground analog. It is a physical experiment, not a simulation.

**Hardware layout.** The flight unit holds a sample card in the middle of a large flow duct. Fans at the top of the duct draw air through it, and flow straighteners sit at the bottom of the experiment module.

**Samples.** The card carries 9 samples, each 5 cm x 29 cm. Compared with Saffire-I, SAFFIRE-II added SIBAL (cotton-fiberglass), silicone, structure PMMA, flat PMMA, and PMMA with Nomex. The supplied table shows only 5 of 16 rows (four silicone samples of differing thickness and one SIBAL sample).

**Conditions.**
- Air flow velocity of 20 or 30 cm/s depending on the material, either concurrent or opposed to flame spread.
- In the visible rows: 80 W ignition power for 9.2 s, and oxygen of about 21.5 to 22.1 percent. The table refers to a "Note 1" for oxygen that was not supplied.

**Variables.** Independent: material, thickness, flow velocity, flow direction, oxygen level, ignition settings. Dependent: burn time, burn length, spread length or rate, flame size, temperatures, radiation, gas composition, pressure.

**Instruments.**
- Two cameras imaged the flame after ignition.
- Thermocouples measured flow, flame, and plume temperatures.
- Two radiometers recorded flame radiation.
- Solid-state sensors measured pressure and CO2 and O2 mole fraction.
- Two anemometers tracked flow velocity.
- All data were compressed and downlinked.

Further sample and parameter details are said to be in an Experimental Details file that was not supplied.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The investigation states a hypothesis: because fire behavior differs greatly between normal and reduced gravity, using terrestrial fires and standards to design spacecraft fire safety systems carries inherent risk from significant uncertainty. That approach has worked so far, but the source argues the uncertainty and risk will grow as missions travel farther from Earth with longer return times. Microgravity data available to designers are scarce, so the investigation proposed large-scale fire experiments in an expendable spacecraft without crew risk.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

The supplied material contains an experimental table (5 of 16 rows) and publication titles, but no narrative results or the publications' content.

**Experimentally observed (from visible table rows only).**
- All four silicone samples (0.27 to 1.03 mm, concurrent and opposed flow, 20 cm/s) showed insignificant burn time and a microgravity burn length of about 0.
- The SIBAL sample (sample 2-5, concurrent, 20 cm/s) burned for 145 s with a burn length of 29 cm, the full sample length. A value of 2.1 mm/s appears in the column headed "Spread Length", which looks like a spread rate; the heading appears mislabeled.
- The table also lists 1-g comparison entries. Silicone 0.27 mm burned completely, 0.61 mm burned 7.6 cm at 1.2 mm/s, 1.03 mm burned about 0, and the opposed-flow 2-4 sample burned completely at 0.6 mm/s. The source does not describe how these 1-g values were obtained.

**Computational findings.** None supplied. A listed paper compares a transient flame growth model with experiment for a large fabric, but its results are not included.

**Interpretation.** The visible rows suggest that thin silicone, which burned fully in the 1-g column, did not sustain burning in these microgravity runs at about 21.5 to 22.1 percent O2 and 20 cm/s, while the SIBAL fabric did. This is a reading of five rows, not a conclusion stated by the source, and the remaining 11 rows could change the picture.

**Stated expected contributions.** The source says results determine microgravity flammability limits for several spacecraft materials, help validate NASA's material selection criteria, and show how microgravity and limited oxygen affect flame size.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

- **Material flammability.** Direct comparison of low-g behavior with the normal-gravity screening test bears on whether that test is a reliable guide for spacecraft materials.
- **Flame behavior and growth.** Camera, temperature, and radiometer data target flame growth and spread in large-scale low-speed flow.
- **Oxygen and environment.** Measuring O2, CO2, pressure and flow velocity lets burning be tied to environmental conditions; the source says oxygen limitation affects flame size.
- **Detection.** A listed publication evaluates gas and particle sensors for spacecraft-relevant fire emissions; the source does not say the sensors were part of the flight, so the link is indirect.
- **Modeling.** Data support model-versus-experiment comparison, per the listed publication on fabric flame growth.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

- **Gravity changes flammability rankings (supported in part).** The visible silicone results show a clear 1-g versus microgravity contrast, though the two columns' origins are not fully documented.
- **Material thickness and flow matter (partly supported).** Silicone thickness and flow direction vary across the visible rows, but the flame outcomes there differ mostly between materials and gravity levels.
- **Scale matters (stated motivation).** The experiment was designed for fires larger than crewed experiments allow; related publications address "microgravity and scale," but their conclusions were not supplied.
- **Standards uncertainty (stated hypothesis, not a result).** Whether terrestrial standards are adequate is the question; the supplied data do not settle it.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

The source frames the work as crucial for current and future missions. By testing in an expendable vehicle, it provides a route to realistic fire data relevant to exploration vehicles and long-duration transit. Results could inform material selection criteria and fire safety design for spacecraft, and by extension habitats, but the source does not claim these changes have been implemented, and it does not specifically discuss lunar or Mars habitats.

## 9. Earth Benefits

The source states that studying fire in small, sealed environments such as the Cygnus vehicle benefits fire safety and prevention on Earth, including inside mines, airplanes, and submarines. No other terrestrial applications are supported by the supplied material.
