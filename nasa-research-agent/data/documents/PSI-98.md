# Spacecraft Fire Safety Experiment-I (SAFFIRE-I)

Every fire safety experiment conducted aboard the ISS faces an inescapable constraint: you cannot deliberately burn something large in a crewed spacecraft. The fire has to be small, controlled, and extinguishable within seconds — which means that the most critical question in spacecraft fire safety, how a large-scale fire actually develops in microgravity, has gone unanswered for decades of human spaceflight. SAFFIRE-I found a way around that constraint by doing something that had never been tried before: igniting a genuinely large piece of flammable fabric inside an uncrewed cargo vehicle on its way to burn up in the atmosphere. With no crew aboard and no return mission planned, the Cygnus resupply vehicle became a one-use fire laboratory — the first opportunity in spaceflight history to observe a realistic large-scale fire in microgravity without putting anyone at risk.

SAFFIRE-I represents a conceptual departure from all previous microgravity fire experiments. Rather than constraining the fire to a size safe for a crewed environment, the investigation burned a sample nearly one meter long aboard an unoccupied spacecraft — a scale previously impossible within the bounds of human spaceflight safety.

---

## 2. Research Objectives

The safety of crewed spacecraft has long depended on a combination of careful material selection and conservative flammability standards derived from normal-gravity tests. What has been largely missing from that framework is direct experimental evidence of how large fires — not centimeter-scale laboratory samples — actually behave in the microgravity conditions those materials will encounter in service.

SAFFIRE-I targeted two specific gaps in that evidence base:

1. **Fire growth rate at scale:** How rapidly does a large fire develop in low gravity? Growth rate governs how quickly a fire becomes uncontrollable, how much time the crew has to respond, and what suppressant delivery rate would be required to arrest the fire before it reaches a critical size. No previous microgravity experiment had addressed this at anything close to realistic scale.

2. **Low-gravity flammability limits compared to the NASA standard:** NASA's material flammability screening test (NASA-STD-6001 Test 1) is a normal-gravity upward flame spread test. SAFFIRE-I sought to determine whether the flammability limits it establishes — the conditions under which a material will or will not support flame spread — correspond to what actually happens in microgravity, or whether the gravity dependence of the test introduces systematic error into the material selection process.

## 3. Experimental Approach

### Experimental Platform

SAFFIRE-I was conducted aboard the **Orbital Sciences Cygnus** cargo resupply vehicle after it had completed its ISS docking mission and been released for destructive reentry. With the vehicle uncrewed and committed to atmospheric disposal, the fire experiment was performed during the period between ISS departure and reentry — a window of opportunity that made large-scale fire testing possible without any safety risk to the station or its crew.

### Fuel Sample

The test material was **SIBAL fabric** — a composite of 75% cotton and 25% fiberglass by weight. The same material appeared in the BASS investigation (PSI-26) and in related ground-based studies. Its selection reflects a practical balance: cotton burns readily and predictably, while the fiberglass substrate maintains the sample's structural integrity after the cotton is consumed, preventing the sample from collapsing in a way that would disrupt the flame spread geometry. The SAFFIRE-I sample was approximately **0.94 m long and 0.40 m wide** — large enough to constitute a genuinely hazardous fire in any inhabited confined space.

### Flow Duct and Airflow

The sample was mounted on a card installed in the center of a **large flow duct** within the Cygnus enclosure. Air was drawn through the duct by fans at the top, with flow straighteners at the bottom ensuring a uniform velocity profile across the sample. SAFFIRE-I was conducted at a controlled airflow velocity of **20 cm/s** — a velocity intended to simulate the kind of forced ventilation flow present in a spacecraft environmental control system.

Two flow configurations were tested:

- **Concurrent flow (Sample S1):** Air flowing in the same direction as flame spread, creating conditions where hot combustion products are advected ahead of the flame front and preheat unburned fuel.
- **Opposed flow (Sample S2):** Air flowing against the direction of flame spread, limiting fuel preheating and tending to slow or extinguish the spreading flame.

### Instrumentation

The instrument suite was comprehensive for a vehicle of this type:

- **Two cameras** per sample, capturing flame images from different angles throughout the burn.
- **Thermocouples** monitoring flow temperature, flame temperature, and plume temperature.
- **Two radiometers** recording flame radiation in real time.
- **Solid-state sensors** measuring ambient pressure, CO₂ mole fraction, and O₂ mole fraction — allowing the depletion of cabin oxygen and the accumulation of combustion products to be tracked.
- **Two anemometers** tracking actual flow velocity within the duct during the burn.

All data was compressed and downlinked to ground controllers during the experiment window.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The underlying position of SAFFIRE-I is stated as a risk hypothesis rather than a narrowly defined scientific prediction: **the significant differences between fire behavior in normal and reduced gravity mean that relying on terrestrial fire standards and Earth-based test data to design spacecraft fire safety systems introduces inherent uncertainty and risk.** While that approach had not resulted in a catastrophic fire event up to the time of the investigation, the argument was that the uncertainty itself represents an unacceptable unknown — particularly as missions extend further from Earth and emergency return becomes measured in days or months rather than hours.

The specific experimental prediction embedded in this position is that large-scale fire behavior in microgravity will differ meaningfully from both small-scale microgravity tests (which were the only alternative available before SAFFIRE) and normal-gravity large-scale tests (which were the only large-scale reference previously available). Testing that prediction required a fire experiment at a scale and duration that no prior investigation had been able to achieve.

## 5. Findings and Scientific Contributions

> **Note:** The PSI record provided does not include a results summary. The experimental table is complete at five entries, covering the two primary burn samples and three pre-test flow visualization runs. Quantitative fire growth data, spread rates, and radiation measurements are contained in the associated publications. No results have been fabricated or inferred beyond what the supplied PSI material and cited publication titles indicate.

### Unprecedented Scale of Microgravity Fire Data

The most immediate contribution of SAFFIRE-I is the existence of the dataset itself. With a sample nearly a meter long and burn durations of up to 420 seconds (seven minutes) for the concurrent-flow case, SAFFIRE-I generated microgravity fire data at a scale and duration with no prior equivalent. This dataset provides a physical ground truth against which existing small-scale microgravity results and normal-gravity large-scale models can be compared.

### Concurrent vs. Opposed Flow Comparison

The sharp difference in burn time between the concurrent-flow test (420 s) and the opposed-flow test (70 s) is immediately visible in the experimental table, even without full results. This contrast illustrates the fundamental role of airflow direction in determining fire persistence in microgravity — concurrent flow sustains and grows the fire, while opposed flow limits it severely. The quantitative characterization of this relationship at operational scale is directly relevant to spacecraft ventilation design.

### Transient Fire Growth Modelling

Associated publications document both experimental observations and computational modelling of transient flame growth and spread over the large SIBAL sample under concurrent low-speed flow in microgravity. The comparison between model predictions and SAFFIRE-I experimental data provided a direct validation test for fire spread models at a scale not previously accessible to microgravity experimenters.

### Flammability Limit Validation at Operational Scale

By operating at ambient oxygen concentrations close to the standard atmospheric value (~21.5%) and measuring how the flame evolved over time, SAFFIRE-I contributed evidence relevant to validating — or potentially challenging — the flammability limits established by NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 for the SIBAL material. Whether the material's behavior in the large-scale microgravity environment is consistent with what the small-sample normal-gravity test predicts is a central scientific question the investigation was designed to answer.

### Enabling Future SAFFIRE Investigations

SAFFIRE-I established the hardware concept, operational approach, and data collection methodology for a series of successive investigations (PSI-99, PSI-100), each burning aboard a subsequent Cygnus vehicle. The first investigation therefore carries the additional scientific value of proving that large-scale uncrewed fire experiments are feasible and can return useful data — a programmatic contribution as important as any individual measurement.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

**Category: prevention**

SAFFIRE-I is among the most directly fire-safety-relevant investigations in the PSI program. It addresses the problem of large-scale fire behavior in microgravity as an engineering reality rather than a theoretical concern.

- **Fire growth rate at operational scale:** Knowing how fast a large fire grows in microgravity determines the available response time for crew action and automatic suppression. SAFFIRE-I measured this for the first time at a physically meaningful scale.
- **Flammability limits and material selection:** The investigation's comparison of microgravity large-scale flammability behavior with normal-gravity test standards directly challenges or supports the adequacy of existing material certification criteria for spacecraft use.
- **Airflow direction as a fire safety parameter:** The concurrent vs. opposed flow comparison demonstrates that the direction of environmental control system airflow relative to a fire can be the difference between a seven-minute burn and a seventy-second one — a potentially critical factor in spacecraft cabin design.
- **Oxygen and CO₂ tracking during a real fire:** The in-situ measurement of O₂ depletion and CO₂ accumulation during a large fire in a sealed cabin environment provides data relevant to understanding how rapidly the atmosphere in an enclosed spacecraft becomes hazardous during a fire event.
- **Smoke and combustion product characterization:** Radiometer and gas composition data from SAFFIRE-I support fire detection system design by characterising what sensors would actually measure during a realistic large-scale cabin fire.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

### Small-Sample Microgravity Tests Do Not Fully Represent Large-Scale Fire Behavior
The existence of SAFFIRE-I as a program is itself an acknowledgment of this insight. The investigation was premised on the recognition that centimeter-scale samples in drop towers or ISS gloveboxes — while scientifically valuable — cannot replicate the physics of a fire large enough to threaten a spacecraft. Scale affects flame residence times, radiative interactions between the flame and the fuel surface, oxygen depletion dynamics, and the structural integrity of the burning sample in ways that do not simply extrapolate from small-scale tests.

### Airflow Direction Is a First-Order Fire Safety Variable
The 6:1 ratio in burn times between the concurrent-flow and opposed-flow tests at otherwise identical conditions illustrates that airflow direction is not a minor modifier of fire behavior — it is a primary determinant of whether a fire grows to a dangerous size or self-limits. For spacecraft designers, this means that environmental control system airflow patterns are a fire safety design parameter, not merely a comfort parameter.

### The Normal-Gravity Standard May Carry Uncharacterized Uncertainty
By directly measuring large-scale microgravity flammability, SAFFIRE-I created the first empirical basis for assessing how much error is introduced when normal-gravity small-sample test results are used to certify spacecraft materials. The investigation does not render the existing standard invalid — but it provides the evidence needed to understand where the standard is conservative, where it may not be, and what changes might improve it.

### Uncrewed Expendable Vehicles Are Viable Fire Research Platforms
The SAFFIRE approach — using a vehicle committed to atmospheric disposal as a test article — is a genuinely novel experimental concept. It resolves the fundamental tension between wanting large-scale fire data and not being able to create large-scale fires in crewed spacecraft. This concept opened a research pathway that has been continued in PSI-99 and PSI-100.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

### Crew Safety on Long-Duration Missions
On a mission to Mars, an emergency return to Earth takes months. Any fire safety protocol that relies on "get back quickly if something burns" is not viable. SAFFIRE-I provides the fire growth rate and flammability data needed to design fire safety systems — detection, suppression, atmosphere management — that can handle a large fire autonomously or with crew action alone, without ground support or emergency return as a fallback.

### Material Selection for Future Exploration Vehicles
The comparison between SAFFIRE-I data and NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 results directly informs whether the current material certification process is adequate for vehicles that will operate further from Earth and at potentially different atmospheric compositions. If the investigation reveals discrepancies between normal-gravity test predictions and microgravity large-scale behavior, future material specifications can be adjusted accordingly.

### Environmental Control System Design
The finding that airflow direction is a dominant variable in fire growth rate has direct implications for how cabin ventilation systems are designed and controlled in emergency scenarios. Future spacecraft may implement airflow reversal or selective fan shutdown as a fire response strategy — a protocol whose effectiveness depends on knowing how the fire responds to opposed versus concurrent flow, which SAFFIRE-I measured directly.

### Fire Response Procedures and Training
SAFFIRE-I data on fire growth timelines, oxygen depletion rates, and CO₂ accumulation in a sealed environment provides the physical basis for realistic fire emergency procedure development. Training scenarios and decision thresholds for evacuation or shelter-in-place actions can be grounded in actual large-scale microgravity data rather than extrapolations from small samples.

## 9. Earth Benefits

### Fire Safety in Confined, Sealed Environments
The problem SAFFIRE-I addresses — a large fire in a sealed, ventilation-controlled environment with limited egress options — is directly analogous to fire in submarines, aircraft, mine tunnels, and hyperbaric facilities. Fire behavior in these environments shares critical features with spacecraft fires: limited oxygen supply, sealed gas volumes, forced ventilation flows, and evacuation constraints. SAFFIRE-I data and the models validated against it are therefore directly applicable to improving fire safety standards in these terrestrial settings.

### Performance-Based Fire Standards Development
The SAFFIRE-I approach — measuring actual fire behavior and comparing it to existing standards — provides a template for developing performance-based fire standards that are grounded in physical fire growth data rather than simplified screening tests. This approach is increasingly favored in building fire engineering and could be extended to other confined environments where current standards may carry hidden uncertainty.

### Combustion Model Validation at Large Scale
Computational fire models validated against SAFFIRE-I data at large scale in a well-controlled microgravity environment are more physically reliable tools for predicting fire behavior in complex terrestrial scenarios. The absence of buoyancy in SAFFIRE-I removes one of the most computationally challenging terms in fire dynamics modelling, making the validation exercise particularly discriminating for the physics being tested.

### Improved Understanding of Oxygen-Depleted Fire Behavior
The in-situ O₂ and CO₂ monitoring during a large sealed-volume fire provides data relevant to understanding how fires behave as they consume available oxygen — a process relevant to any sealed-compartment fire scenario on Earth. The transition from oxygen-sufficient to oxygen-limited combustion affects both flame behavior and the toxicity of combustion products, and SAFFIRE-I generated this data in a physically clean environment.

## 10. Limitations and Open Questions

- **Only one material tested (SIBAL).** The entire SAFFIRE-I experimental program used a single composite fabric. How the large-scale microgravity flammability behavior of other materials — thermoplastics, foams, wire insulation, structural composites — differs from SIBAL is not addressed within this investigation.
- **Only one airflow velocity tested (20 cm/s).** A single flow speed was used across all tests. How fire growth rate and flammability limits vary across the range of velocities present in real spacecraft environmental control systems is a question left for subsequent investigations.
- **No associated modeling table.** Despite transient flame growth modeling being a key output of companion publications, no computational dataset is associated with the PSI-98 record.
- **No results summary in the PSI record.** Flame spread rates, radiative fractions, peak temperatures, and oxygen depletion timelines are in the associated publications rather than consolidated in the PSI entry.
- **Single use of the experimental platform.** The Cygnus vehicle is not recoverable, and the experiment could not be repeated with modified conditions using the same hardware. Each test configuration consumed an entire sample and a portion of the available experimental window.
- **Atmospheric composition limited to near-ambient oxygen.** Tests were conducted near 21.5% O₂. Future spacecraft may operate at reduced oxygen concentrations as a fire risk mitigation strategy; how SIBAL and other materials behave at reduced O₂ at large scale in microgravity was not addressed in SAFFIRE-I.
- **Limited to fabric geometry.** The flat fabric sample in a uniform duct flow is a simplified geometry. Real spacecraft fires may involve cables, rigid plastic components, or mixed-material assemblies in irregular compartments where the flow field is far from uniform.
