# Spacecraft Fire Experiment-III (SAFFIRE-III)

SAFFIRE-III is a large-scale, free-flying fire experiment run inside an expendable cargo vehicle, the Orbital Sciences Cygnus resupply spacecraft, after it left the International Space Station. Because no crew is aboard, researchers can burn a sample far larger than anything permitted inside a crewed module. The investigation targets two questions that spacecraft designers currently cannot answer well from ground data: how quickly a fire grows in low gravity, and how the low-gravity flammability limits compare with those from NASA's normal-gravity material flammability screening test.

## 2. Research Objectives

The investigation set out to:

1. Determine how rapidly a large-scale fire grows in low gravity.
2. Investigate low-gravity flammability limits and compare them with those obtained in NASA's normal-gravity material flammability screening test.

The underlying problem is that spacecraft fire safety is designed largely from terrestrial fire behavior and standards, even though fires behave very differently in normal and reduced gravity. Little microgravity data exists for designers to build on. SAFFIRE-III aims to supply large-scale data that can only be obtained safely in an uncrewed vehicle.

## 3. Experimental Approach

**Platform.** The experiments were performed on the Cygnus resupply vehicle after it deberthed from the ISS, so no crew was exposed to risk.

**Sample material.** SIBAL, the same sample material used in SAFFIRE-I. (The supplied material does not expand the acronym or give sample dimensions; these are said to be in an "Experimental Details" file that was not provided.)

**Conditions.** Flow velocity of 30 cm/s, compared with 20 cm/s in SAFFIRE-I. Flow was either concurrent (same direction as flame spread) or opposed.

**Procedure and measurements.** Once the sample was ignited:

- Two cameras captured flame images.
- Thermocouples provided flow, flame, and plume temperatures.
- Two radiometers recorded flame radiation.
- Solid-state sensors measured pressure and CO2 and O2 mole fraction.
- Two anemometers tracked flow velocity.

All data were compressed and downlinked.

**Variables.** Independent: flow direction (concurrent vs. opposed) and the sample/experimental parameters listed in the unprovided Experimental Details file. Dependent: flame growth and appearance (video), temperatures, flame radiation, pressure, CO2 and O2 mole fraction, and flow velocity.

## 4. Research Hypothesis

The stated hypothesis is that relying on terrestrial fires and fire standards to design spacecraft fire safety systems carries inherent risk, because of the significant uncertainty created by the large differences between normal- and reduced-gravity fire behavior. The approach has worked so far, but the source argues that uncertainty and risk grow as exploration missions travel farther from Earth, with much longer transit times for a safe return. Conducting large-scale fire experiments in an expendable spacecraft is proposed as the way to close this data gap.

## 6. Fire Safety Relevance

- **Material flammability:** The comparison of low-gravity flammability limits with the normal-gravity screening test bears directly on whether current material selection criteria are adequate.
- **Flame behavior and fire growth:** Large-scale growth rate in low gravity is a core measurement, with video, temperature and radiation data.
- **Environmental conditions:** O2 and CO2 mole fraction, pressure, and flow velocity are recorded, and the source notes the effect of limited oxygen on flame size.
- **Flow effects:** Concurrent and opposed flow at 30 cm/s probe how forced ventilation influences burning.

The record does not address detection or suppression performance, so no claims are made there.

## 7. Key Scientific Insights

- **Scale matters (design intent).** Large samples in a crew-free vehicle reach conditions that small, crewed-environment tests cannot. This is a rationale in the source, not a demonstrated result.
- **Continuity with SAFFIRE-I.** Using the same material with a higher flow (30 vs. 20 cm/s) allows the effect of flow velocity to be examined, though no comparison outcome is supplied.
- **Standards validation.** The investigation is framed as a test of whether normal-gravity screening reliably predicts low-gravity flammability. The answer is not in the supplied material.
- **Instrumentation breadth.** Combined imaging, temperature, radiation, gas composition and flow data would support later model validation. This is a potential implication.

## 8. Applications in Space Exploration

The source states the investigation is crucial to the safety of current and future missions. Its potential contribution is to give spacecraft designers real microgravity data for fire safety design and procedures, especially for exploration missions with long transit times and no quick return. The record names the ISS-visiting Cygnus vehicle only; it does not discuss lunar or Mars habitats specifically. Nothing here indicates that results have already been incorporated into designs.

## 9. Earth Benefits

The source states that studying fire in small, sealed environments such as the Cygnus vehicle benefits fire safety and prevention on Earth, including inside mines, airplanes, and submarines.
